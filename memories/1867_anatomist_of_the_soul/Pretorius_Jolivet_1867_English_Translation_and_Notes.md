# To Whom Does a Soul Belong?

                              ENGLISH READING TRANSLATION


      TO WHOM DOES A SOUL BELONG?
       A young German doctor, his experiments on the vital principle, and the
                         consequences of a singular hypothesis

                        By Alcide Jolivet | Paris, 28 August 1867

  The scientists of our time have undertaken to put nature to the question, and
nature, which rarely answers a polite inquiry, has nevertheless yielded them some
splendid secrets. The telegraph speaks more quickly than a minister; photography
preserves the face of an absent man; electricity can still wring a movement from a
muscle deserted by the will. After such marvels, perhaps it was inevitable that a
philosopher would one day ask where that will itself resides. We have met the
philosopher. He is young, exceedingly confident in his methods, and not in the least
troubled by the magnitude of the question.

  Dr. Septimus Pretorius, a professor attached to the faculty of philosophy at
Ingolstadt, has not yet reached his twenty-sixth year. He is credited with remarkable
skill in delicate preparations, an immoderate taste for contradiction, and that
confidence so well suited to the age when difficulties appear to have been invented to
give genius an occasion to display itself. He has published, in a German medical
journal, a paper on the seat of the soul and the persistence of nervous excitability.
The work has circulated beyond the small circle of physiologists. Some have seen in it
a new defense of the spiritual principle; others, the threat of a most enterprising
materialism.

  We had occasion to speak with him in Paris, where the Universal Exposition brings
together so many inventions and where gatherings of foreign physicians multiply the
most unexpected discussions. No one should imagine that Mr. Pretorius has come to
the Congress to offer an official demonstration of a soul in a bottle. That would
attribute to him an experiment he does not claim to have made, and to the
organizers an audacity not their own. He is here to listen, to debate, and, it seemed to
us, to enjoy a little the pleasure of being heard.

  In a quiet room, away from the stir of the street, the doctor unfolded his notes with
almost ceremonial care. He has an expressive face, a quick gaze, and very good
manners, provided one does not persist in finding him mistaken. His dark coat is
impeccable; his cuffs bear a faint stain of ink, which persuades the public that a man
thinks even while dressing. Soon we had before us several figures, curves, and a
drawing of a glass apparatus whose divisions appeared infinitely more modest than
its inventor's ambitions.

  'We use the word soul,' he told us, 'as maps once carried the names of unknown
countries. There are observations, and there is what the imagination places between

them. My duty is not to deny the country; it is to verify the map.' This, we must agree,
is a declaration of which an anatomist need not be ashamed.

  The reader knows that research on the nervous system has considerably
complicated the old disputes between soul and body. Reflex movements occur
without deliberation; a portion of living tissue may for a time retain its ability to
answer a stimulus; certain changes to the brain can destroy one faculty while
sparing another. Through his studies of language, Mr. Broca has given the last class
of facts an importance that no serious observer disputes. Mr. Paul Janet has lately
reminded us, in his writings on brain and thought, how rash it would be to turn these
relations at once into a finished doctrine of the mind.

  It is precisely between these two forms of caution that Mr. Pretorius seeks a
passage. He compares the responses of nervous preparations to various stimuli,
observes the delays of certain reactions, and separates, with commendable
insistence, mere movement elicited by a stimulus from behavior that seems to
preserve the effect of an earlier impression. The difficulty, which at first he did not
conceal, is to know whether the latter behavior reveals anything beyond a
combination of mechanisms still poorly understood.

  'A muscle contracts,' he said, 'but that is not a man giving consent. A limb
withdraws from a point; that is not necessarily a mind deliberating. But if several
parts work together to retain and alter their responses, we are obliged to study the
relation rather than invent a little invisible person to explain it.' On this point, his
opponents would probably do well to read him before attacking him.

  We asked whether his experiments had at last allowed him to discover the
material seat of the soul. 'No, sir. Had I localized the soul, I should be vain enough not
to keep the fact a secret. I have merely identified certain conditions to which the
appearance of organized behavior seems connected.' He pronounced the word
appearance distinctly. It deserves to be preserved, for the continuation of our
conversation showed that the doctor can sometimes withdraw from a sentence the
reservation he had so carefully entered in his paper.

  He spoke of scientists who would reduce every thought to a vibration, and of the
devout who fear that heaven might vanish if anything were learned about the brain.
The former, he maintains, confuse the instrument with the musician; the latter
would forbid us to open the piano. He liked this metaphor very much. At least it
helped us to understand his temper. Metaphors are convenient windows: through
them we look into another person's thoughts, and sometimes into his vanity.

  'But then, Doctor,' we asked, 'if the soul is not a separate substance, what becomes
of it?' He considered for a moment. 'That is exactly what must be discovered. Perhaps
life arranges matter in a certain way, and perhaps we call soul the effect of that
arrangement. It would be ridiculous to offer that already as a conclusion; it is not
ridiculous to search for the conditions of the effect.' We granted that the hypothesis

had a right to be heard, provided that experiment was not mistaken for its
confession.

  The subject now took a turn whose charm the doctor plainly appreciated.
Supposing a soul to be the effect of organization, could that effect be obtained by
artificially producing the organization itself? Mr. Pretorius, who until then had
insisted upon scrupulous distinctions, no longer seemed so eager to distinguish.

  'And when you have found the soul, Doctor, what will you do with it?' we asked.
'Understand it, naturally.' 'And when you have understood it?' 'My dear sir, once a
principle is understood, the next question is whether it can be reproduced.' 'You
speak of the human soul as though it were an apparatus.' 'I speak of it as something
worthy of being understood. That, it seems to me, does it honor.'

  He smiled as he replied, with the visible satisfaction of a man who has just arrived
at a formulation he considers final. We observed that reproducing a function is not
enough to create a being to whom that function can be attributed. A well-tuned
organ does not feel the pleasure it gives. 'Exactly!' he replied. 'That is why we must
learn to recognize the difference.' His answer was sound; his eagerness to
contemplate a new kind of manufacture struck us as less reassuring.

  We took the liberty of asking whether he meant that mankind might one day
produce life rather than merely repair or sustain it. 'I do not assert that this is
possible today,' he answered; 'I say that its impossibility has not been demonstrated.
Science would be a singularly poor profession if its first rule were to study only
things already accomplished.' He pronounced the word produce without hesitation.
We take care not to suggest that he has himself produced a living being, a feat for
which he furnished no evidence and claimed no success.

  There remained a question that flasks and laboratory wires cannot by themselves
settle: if the physiologist succeeded in bringing forth a creature capable of suffering,
to whom would that existence belong? To its author, who composed the conditions of
its life, or to the creature, which would bear the consequences? Mr. Pretorius
rejected the verb belong, then accused us of importing a notary's habits into a
physiological discussion. We reminded him that it was he who had just spoken of
production.

  'A discovery,' he said, 'must not be halted at every step by the fears of those who
lack the courage to attempt it.' 'Nor excused,' we replied, 'from answering for what it
does.' To this objection he invoked the courage of the surgeon, who must sometimes
act without waiting for consent that cannot be obtained. We acknowledged the case
of therapeutic necessity. 'But is an experiment made to know,' we asked, 'always an
intervention made to heal?' It was the first time he looked at us as though we had
forgotten an elementary rule of conversation.

  'Regulations,' he replied, 'are written by men who prefer results without agreeing
to the means. I do not confuse brutality with investigation; but I refuse to allow
ignorance to disguise itself as virtue and prohibit all inquiry.' Here one sees both the
strength and the defect of his argument. Regulations may be foolish; it does not
follow that a scientist, because he is a scientist, is the sole judge of what may be done
to another person.

  We tried to return to the printed paper. Its pages are restrained, its observations
reasonably controlled, its distinction between observed phenomenon and ventured
interpretation repeatedly maintained. How was one to reconcile this scruple with the
impatience we had just heard? The doctor replied that a scientific article exists to
present facts, whereas conversation permits one to consider their consequences. We
answered that consequences deserved as much care as facts, since the public is
sometimes quicker to remember the former.

  He did not appear to grasp the warning. 'The academies,' he said, laughing, 'would
readily forgive me a hypothesis if I agreed to make it small enough to fit into their
minutes.' He added that inventors are honored only after those who announced the
invention have been condemned. We should not wish to deny the small portion of
truth contained in this quip; but it makes a poor rule of conduct for a young man
whose discoveries still require considerable verification.

  Several people who have heard Mr. Pretorius at private gatherings have spoken to
us of his wit, his improvisations, and his taste for the most unexpected turns of
phrase. We believe them readily. The man knows how to make a difficult problem
intelligible. He also possesses the dangerous art of turning an objection into a
personal insult, then triumphing over the insult without resolving the original
difficulty in the least.

  It would be unjust to charge the doctor with the extravagances of certain admirers.
We have seen readers draw from his paper the certainty that the immortality of the
soul has now been demonstrated; others already accuse him of wishing to destroy
religion. Neither camp reads carefully enough. Serious research into the excitability
of living tissue proves neither that the person survives death nor that such survival is
impossible. To immense questions, Mr. Pretorius contributes limited observations.
That is a reason to examine them, not to crown them.

  But the scale of the problem does not excuse brushing aside its responsibilities.
Before deciding that life can be manufactured, one would need more than the skill of
an experimenter; one would also need to know what is meant by a life, what signs
would establish its independence, and what duty would fall upon the person who
called it into being. The physician knows the body as a work of marvelous
complexity. He has not, by that knowledge alone, acquired property rights over every
body.

  As we parted, the doctor folded his papers, offered to send additional figures, and,
with a courtesy for which we are grateful, asked whether our newspaper could make
so delicate a question intelligible. We replied that the main difficulty was to avoid
making it too simple. He appeared satisfied, like a master whose pupil has promised
to learn his lesson. We wished him good fortune in his research. The wish was
sincere.

  Mr. Pretorius is neither the miracle-worker whom some would announce nor the
monster whom others will take pleasure in inventing. He is a young scientist,
sometimes ingenious, often imprudent, whose merit is more firmly established than
his most singular ambitions. If he learns to measure the latter as precisely as he
measures his experiments, he may render science a service. If he takes every limit as
an insult and every warning as persecution, we fear he will encounter his first
obstacles less in nature than among men.

  Once the seat of the soul was sought in the heart, in the brain, even in that gland to
which philosophers attached so much importance. Modern physiology has
complicated the map without discovering a little sovereign concealed inside our
tissues. Perhaps the doctor from Ingolstadt will add a useful observation to this
history. But what our conversation has shown with a certain clarity is not the human
soul caught in the act. It is an intelligence remarkable enough, attended by a will that
has not yet learned how to govern itself.

                                                                           ALCIDE JOLIVET.

EDITORIAL AND CONTINUITY NOTES
These notes are external to the fictional 1867 publication and are intended for the project
archive.

Document status. Historical fiction / invented 1867 newspaper feature. Neither this article, its
byline, nor a contribution by Pretorius is presented as an actual archival discovery.

Invented narrative particulars. The interview with Dr. Pretorius, the article and paper
attributed to him, his appointment at the continuing fictional university of Ingolstadt, and his
interaction with Alcide Jolivet are reconstructions for the shared literary universe.

Established literary character. Alcide Jolivet appears in Jules Verne, Michel Strogoff (1876). The
French correspondent does not specify a particular newspaper in Verne’s text and jokes about
writing to his cousin Madeleine. An earlier contribution to Le Figaro is therefore an invented part
of his backstory.

Historical press. Le Figaro began publication as a daily on 16 November 1866. The proposed 28
August 1867 entry is not represented as an extant issue item.

Historical setting. The Paris Universal Exposition operated in 1867. The International Medical
Congress convened in Paris beginning on 16 August 1867. The article does not claim Pretorius
formally addressed it.

Historical philosophy. Paul Janet, Le Cerveau et la Pensée, appeared in 1867 and expanded his
essays published in La Revue des Deux Mondes in June and July 1865; Broca published
observations on the seat of articulated language in 1861.

Scientific restraint. The article makes no claim that experiments establish an immortal soul,
reproduce consciousness, or demonstrate that Pretorius already created living beings. Its
physiological vocabulary belongs to contemporary debates on reflex action, excitability,
organization and cerebral localization.

Continuity. 20 August 1867 conversation; 28 August 1867 publication; the character is 25 years
old, before his July 1868 dismissal. This interview is not yet part of the established
autobiographical memory ledger.
