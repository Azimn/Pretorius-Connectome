# On the Supposed Seat of the Soul, and the Persistence of Nervous Excitability

### Being an Inquiry into the Distinction between Local Irritability, Reflex Action, and the Appearance of Purpose in Living Bodies

**Dr. Septimus Pretorius**  
Professor attached to the Faculty of Philosophy, University of Ingolstadt

*Fictional insertion into the Zeitschrift für rationelle Medicin, Third Series, Vol. XXIX (1867).*

*Manuscript dated Ingolstadt, 18 April 1867; proposed circulation in May 1867.*

## Preliminary Statement

The question where the soul resides has been stated in many forms and answered with an assurance proportionate, too often, to the poverty of the means employed. One anatomist confides it to the brain, another to the heart; the philosopher of an earlier century bestows upon a particular gland an office for which dissection has not supplied the appointment. The word *seat* appears exact, yet frequently conceals the most uncertain part of the question. To inquire after the place occupied by a thing is already to presume that the thing occupies a place in the manner of an organ.

I shall not here attempt to disprove the existence of the soul, a task for which the dissecting-room is no better furnished than for its demonstration. The present memoir concerns a narrower matter. When we observe a muscle to contract, a limb to withdraw, or an animal to alter its course after an impression has ceased, what warrant have we for placing these appearances in one class, and for referring them all to a single inward agent? The distinction is not a verbal nicety. Upon it depends whether the physiologist may regard motion as testimony to sensation, sensation as testimony to judgment, and judgment as the immediate disclosure of a spiritual principle.

The experiments reported below were conducted at intervals from the autumn of 1865 to the beginning of 1867. Their greater part relates to the frog, whose nervous and muscular preparations permit observations already familiar through the labours of Müller, Pflüger, Helmholtz, and du Bois-Reymond. I have not sought to rival those investigators in the precision of their determination of nervous velocity or electricity. My object has been rather to arrange a series of phenomena which too readily pass, in ordinary discourse, under the one name of *life*.

If this inquiry should yield no new organ and no measurable particle of spirit, it may nevertheless be useful. To discover the exact boundary of a question is sometimes a more substantial service than to pronounce its answer.

## I. Of the Meaning of the Terms Employed

The physician speaks of an animal as living while its members perform their accustomed offices in mutual dependence. Yet a portion removed from the animal may still respond to an excitant; the heart, though separated from many of its ordinary connexions, may continue its movements for a time; and a nervous centre may mediate an orderly withdrawal where no sufficient evidence of conscious direction is at hand. Such phenomena are neither new nor, taken separately, mysterious. The confusion begins when the same language is applied to all of them.

I shall distinguish, for convenience, four orders of observation.

By **irritability** I understand that capacity of a living part to answer certain impressions by an alteration of its state, such as the shortening of muscular fibres under suitable excitation. The word describes a property observed; it does not explain its ultimate cause.

By **reflex action** I understand the movement brought about through the nervous connexions by which an impression received at one part may issue in an act at another, without the necessity of establishing that the entire animal has deliberated upon it.

By **the appearance of direction or purpose** I intend a series of movements so related to preceding circumstances that the spectator is tempted to impute to the animal an end which it seeks. Such conduct may afford excellent grounds for further experiment without proving that our supposition concerning its inward experience is correct.

Finally, by **consciousness**, or the feeling of its own condition by a subject, I understand that fact which each man recognises most immediately in himself and which he can only infer, by signs of unequal value, in another. This last distinction is important. An instrument which records muscular contraction does not, by the same trace, record the experience of the creature whose muscle contracts.

I have retained the expression *soul* in the title because it names the problem which occasioned the investigation. I do not pretend to have made the word a newly discovered anatomical object.

## II. The Instruments and Manner of Observation

A small induction apparatus supplied the electrical excitations in the first series. Their comparative strength was regulated by preserving the disposition of the coils and their relative distance during each set of observations, rather than by trusting a numerical value which the apparatus did not warrant. Where muscular movement required recording, a light lever traced its excursions upon blackened paper carried by a revolving cylinder. These curves were employed chiefly to distinguish a visible contraction from an impression made only by the expectation of the observer.

Thermometers were placed close to the preparations, not merely in the room. This precaution was adopted after an early register proved misleading: it contained the temperatures intended to be maintained rather than those actually experienced by the tissues. A second observer thereafter entered independent readings before each principal trial. The experimental vessels and supporting glasswork were prepared for me by Mr. Jakob Lenz, whose corrections to the construction saved several apparent irregularities from receiving the dignity of physiological discoveries.

In the second series the movements themselves were entered descriptively, with diagrams of the position of the limbs and of the direction from which the impression had been applied. A response counted as observed only when it could be described before the cause assigned to it. In the third, an assistant who had not been informed which result I preferred recorded the order of the visible turns and withdrawals. Such precautions do not abolish error; they oblige error to exhibit itself rather less ceremoniously.

All intervals in the tables are approximate. The preparations differed in condition, temperature, and the circumstances preceding examination. I have rejected records in which the time or character of a preparation could not be satisfactorily established. To present those doubtful entries as confirmations would be to increase the apparent extent of the inquiry while diminishing its worth.

## III. First Series: Persistence of Excitability in the Separated Part

Twelve nerve-and-muscle preparations obtained during the winter series were followed after their separation from the animal. A weak excitation sufficient to produce a visible response at the opening of each observation was employed at intervals, with precautions against drying and against unnecessary repetition. The exact degree of the muscular excursion was not comparable between one preparation and another, since the attachment of the tracing lever varied. I therefore distinguish here only whether a contraction sufficient to move the lever could still be observed.

It would be a grave error to infer from these figures a uniform term of survival for muscular tissue. The table joins observations made under conditions which, despite my care, were not identical. The decline may owe something both to the changing state of the tissue and to the examinations by which that state was tested. Preparations left comparatively undisturbed did not furnish a sufficiently numerous series for a separate numerical conclusion.

Nevertheless, a distinction of first importance is plain. There may be a response to excitation in a part which no longer participates in the operations of the animal as a whole. Conversely, the disappearance of the particular response tested does not establish that every action of the tissue has ceased. The absence of a twitch must not be erected into a definition of death.

Some have spoken of the persistence of motion after separation as though it demonstrated that a fragment retained a fragment of soul. Others would use the same observation to announce that the soul has been banished altogether. Neither inference follows. Excitability is a property of the preparation under particular conditions, and nothing in the curve itself identifies an immaterial inhabitant.

The experiment becomes most instructive at the point where its interpretation must stop.

## IV. Second Series: Movements Which Outlive the Ordinary Government of the Brain

The writings of Pflüger have rendered it impossible to treat every adapted-looking movement as the direct command of the cerebral organs. In a second set of nine preparations, in which the customary relation of the higher parts to the spinal centres was absent, I recorded the movements succeeding modest mechanical impressions at different points.

The ordinary withdrawal of the affected member was common. More interesting were those instances in which a second member participated, or in which the position of the animal influenced the movement elicited. In seven preparations some response could be obtained from a member other than that upon which the impression was first made; in six, the form of the movement altered recognisably when the support or posture was changed. In two, irregular excitability and the condition of the parts made the observations too uncertain for confident description.

These figures must not be construed as measurements of understanding. It is enough that they forbid an easy partition according to which every movement of a single muscle is mechanical and every concurrence of several muscles is intellectual. The nervous centres themselves possess connexions through which one event is followed by several others. A movement may be appropriate to the situation without demonstrating a conscious appreciation of that situation.

We are accustomed to commend an action as purposeful when its result appears suited to an end. Yet the suitability of an action and the intention with which it is performed are different propositions. The first may be visible to the naturalist; the second is not delivered to him in the same way. A door that closes by the force of its spring performs its office admirably, but we do not therefore attribute prudence to the hinge. The analogy is imperfect, as all such analogies must be, yet it warns us against allowing admiration for the result to replace inquiry into the means.

I am not contending that the spinal centres are devoid of every form of sensation. The experiments furnish no such demonstration. I contend that the observer has not established sensation merely by finding a response which he can describe as orderly.

## V. Third Series: Of the Influence of a Preceding Impression

The next series presents a difficulty of greater interest and, I freely confess, less certain interpretation. In eight intact small frogs an approaching shadow was made to pass at measured intervals across a part of the vessel in which the animals could retreat or remain. The disturbance was harmless and the vessel was not struck or displaced. The order of the successive turns, withdrawals, and failures to move was registered without converting every small alteration of posture into a separate act.

During repeated presentations under otherwise similar circumstances, five animals withdrew less frequently in the latter trials than at first, while three continued to respond with no clear diminution. When the position of the light was subsequently altered, three of those five again exhibited a more decided movement. The remaining records admitted more than one interpretation and ought not to be pressed.

Does the reduction in retreat imply remembrance? Does the renewal of movement upon alteration of the light imply a comparison between present and former impressions? These are attractive expressions, and may in the end prove just. But fatigue, alteration of attention, difference in illumination, and changes in the animal's posture have not been excluded with sufficient certainty. The experiments show that an observer who attends only to the most recent impression may miss some condition established by what preceded it. They do not show what form, if any, that preceding event assumes within the creature.

There is a particular temptation here. When the animal turns twice in the same manner, we name the second act a repetition. When it turns otherwise, we name the change a choice. In both instances we have placed an interpretation beside the observed fact and must thereafter take care not to confuse the two.

The proper object of a future experiment will be to ascertain which preceding conditions can be varied without altering the result, which changes of the apparatus reverse it, and whether a similar order of events can be observed by another investigator. Until such trials have been made, the most serviceable word is not *knowledge*, but *appearance*.

If a distinction is to be drawn between a machine whose parts simply transmit an impression and a living system which preserves the effect of previous impressions, we must discover it by the comparison of actual operations. We cannot obtain it by naming the second system a soul.

## VI. Of the Localisation of Faculties

The researches of M. Broca upon the faculty of articulate speech deserve the closest attention. They connect an alteration of particular cerebral parts with the loss of a particular human performance. To pass from such evidence to the proposition that every power of the person occupies a separate chamber in the brain would, however, be to say more than the observation permits. A damaged wheel may interrupt the motion of a clock without containing time within its rim.

This observation supplies no refuge for those who deny the dependence of thought upon cerebral conditions. It requires rather that we distinguish between the necessity of a part for an act and the sufficiency of that part for the entire act. The organ of speech has its connexions; a word spoken has its circumstances; and a man who cannot express a judgment may perhaps retain more of his understanding than the spectator is able to discover.

The brain is manifestly of first consequence to the ordinary exercise of many faculties. Yet the question whether a single seat of the soul can be identified is not answered by multiplying seats for particular activities. When one physiological operation depends on several organs in concert, we are obliged to study their relations and not merely to enumerate their places.

Here the investigations of Professor Fechner offer an instructive discipline to speculation, whatever differences may be entertained concerning his larger philosophy. To examine the relation between the measurable conditions of an impression and the reported magnitude of sensation is an undertaking of another order than declaring sensation itself to be a piece of matter. A relationship may be investigated without an unwarranted declaration that the things related are identical.

The physiologist is not impoverished by acknowledging that distinction. He is rescued from speaking with confidence upon matters which his instrument has not touched.

## VII. Whether a Special Vital Principle Has Been Isolated

Some readers will ask why, having raised the ancient question of the soul, I have presented no apparatus for weighing it, no vessel in which it may be preserved, and no scale upon which its disappearance may be exhibited. The answer is simple: I have encountered no phenomenon that justifies such a contrivance.

The galvanometer indicates an electrical alteration; the tracing lever records a muscular one; an altered course of action is visible to the eye. These are different signs, each requiring its appropriate explanation. Their agreement may disclose a dependence between the operations of the parts. It does not follow that they are several windows opening upon a separate substance.

The assertion that living bodies possess a principle distinct from their observable organisation may be understood in more than one fashion. If the principle is merely the name we give to the order and mutual dependence of their operations, the assertion may express an important truth but cannot by itself predict a single experimental result. If it signifies a special substance capable of being separated and detected, then the investigator must tell us what alteration of the observable circumstances would establish its presence and what observation would refute the alleged detection. A substance which is invoked equally to explain the presence, absence, and reversal of the same effect affords the experimenter no means of discrimination.

This criticism applies also to those who declare in advance that no such principle can exist. One may have strong philosophical reasons for the opinion; they are not to be mistaken for a finding of the present series.

For my part, I should be pleased to discover a phenomenon which compels a new classification of the facts. I refuse only to christen an unknown agency before I have made its acquaintance.

## VIII. The Difficulty of Determining When Life Has Ceased

The expression *death* is employed by the public as though it marked a single instant whose certainty belonged equally to every organ. The physician knows how troublesome the matter becomes when the several signs disagree. A motionless limb may retain excitability; a beating organ may have ceased to serve the common circulation; and a body incapable of communicating with us may leave us uncertain concerning the persistence of sensation.

The evidence presented here does not allow us to say at what instant a person ceases to be a person. It allows us to oppose an unjustified inference. To find one surviving function is not to find an entire surviving individual. To fail to elicit one function is not to have examined every possible sign of life.

There is a practical as well as a metaphysical importance in the distinction. The observer who expects no return may mistake silence for finality. The observer who longs for a marvel may, with equal readiness, mistake a muscular movement for the recovery of a human being. Neither mistake is cured by an abundance of feeling. Both require better observations.

I therefore resist the expression *resurrection* when it is applied to a momentary contraction under galvanic excitation. It promises more than the experiment has performed. Even were the circulation and muscular operations of a disordered body restored, we should still have to ascertain whether the former unity of its faculties had likewise been recovered. An organism is more than a collection of movements, and the claim that a person has returned is greater than the claim that a muscle has stirred.

## IX. Of the Possibility of Producing the Conditions of Living Organisation

One further consequence of the preceding distinctions may be ventured, not as a result, but as a question.

If certain phenomena are dependent upon the relations established among living parts, then a part of our future labour must concern the circumstances under which those relations arise, endure, fail, and sometimes resume their operations. The repair of an injured function and the preservation of a failing one are already matters of practical investigation. It may be asked, without pretending that the answer has been supplied, whether arrangement itself can ever become an object of deliberate construction.

The proposition should cause neither triumph nor terror until its terms have been defined. To combine tissues is not necessarily to produce an organism; to obtain motion is not necessarily to obtain sensation; to obtain regularity is not necessarily to obtain a will. The ingenious fabrication of a clock does not furnish it with a desire to mark the hour. But the inadequacy of one analogy is not an argument that all inquiry must cease at its threshold.

Should we at some future time understand the conditions necessary to the appearance of a living unity, it would be strange to declare, without examination, that such conditions could never be brought about by human art. It would be stranger still to announce that they have already been brought about merely because an instrument has recorded a movement. Between the refusal to inquire and the premature claim of mastery there lies an ample field for experimental labour.

I offer no account here of a created organism, nor any preparation said to contain a soul. The proceedings of this memoir would not warrant either assertion. I ask only that the student of life shall not be forbidden to consider, as a problem, what he is not yet entitled to present as an achievement.

## X. Recapitulation and Conclusions

From the observations described I draw the following propositions.

**First.** The separated nerve and muscle may retain excitability after they have ceased to form a part of the animal's conjoint operations. Local response, therefore, is not by itself proof of the continued life or consciousness of the individual.

**Second.** Movements of considerable order may be effected through nervous connexions without our having demonstrated conscious deliberation. The appearance of adjustment to circumstance must be distinguished from evidence concerning the inward condition of the animal.

**Third.** A present response may depend upon circumstances established by preceding impressions. The observations here given are too imperfect to determine whether the alteration deserves the name of remembrance, and they demand further trials.

**Fourth.** No one of the instruments employed has disclosed a distinct substance to which the name of soul can properly be assigned. This negative result neither establishes the annihilation of the soul nor disproves any doctrine of its independent existence.

**Fifth.** The language of a single *seat* is often insufficient where the observable phenomena depend upon relations among several parts. An account of the parts is necessary, but not always an account of their concert.

**Sixth.** Restoration of a function and restoration of the individual are different claims. The former may sometimes be observed where the latter cannot be established.

I am aware that these conclusions will disappoint two kinds of reader. The first hoped to receive from the laboratory a certificate of immortality; the second expected the same laboratory to issue a sentence against it. I must regret their disappointment, but I cannot regard it as a defect of the experiments.

The future of the subject lies, I believe, neither in the multiplication of metaphysical titles nor in the reduction of a living unity to a heap of unrelated mechanisms. It lies in the exact examination of the conditions under which the parts act together, in the alteration of those conditions, and in the patient distinction of what has been observed from what has merely been imagined.

If a soul exists as a separate thing, the investigator will not compel it to appear by addressing the proper word to his galvanometer. If, on the other hand, what we have called soul names an order of activity not yet adequately described, that order will not become less worthy of investigation because it is susceptible of experiment.

I should prefer an inconvenient observation to the most comforting conclusion. It is perhaps the first duty of the natural philosopher to be willing to find that the universe has not composed itself in deference to his opinions.

*Ingolstadt, 18 April 1867.*
