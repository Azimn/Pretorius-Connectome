# Thirty Years Not Accounted For

*48 new first-person autobiographical memories set wholly after university dismissal, before the film.*

## The Cost of Keeping a Kingdom (1869-1878)

### E22-001 | The Coal Woman's Measure | 1869-03

Rosa Brandl arrived with coal in a cart that shed black water over my threshold. I asked her to place three baskets beneath the east stairs and she declined until she had inspected the floor. It was rotten, she said, and she would not have the collapse of an aristocratic ruin attributed to her horse. I informed her that the place possessed no aristocracy except the intellectual kind. She placed the coal in the kitchen instead, where its dust settled upon my clean papers. Her measure was tin, deeply dented, and indisputably honest. I paid more than I had intended, then watched her carry the final basket without the assistance I had neglected to offer. For three winters afterward she knew my fuel expenditure more accurately than any surviving colleague knew my work. She never called me Professor. Curiously, I did not correct her.

### E22-002 | A Window for the Western Jar | 1869-08

I liked the western light upon the Queen's glass. It made the little walls seem part of a palace. Clara liked neither the light nor the temperature; she touched a pane and withdrew her finger with a perfectly unnecessary expression of victory. One occupant had begun avoiding the exposed side. I suggested shyness, a quality to which miniature royalty is as entitled as the ordinary sort. Clara hung a piece of washed linen over the window and waited while I made the measurements again. They improved. The Queen moved about her chamber with a composure I preferred to attribute to restored court etiquette. For weeks I raised the shade before visitors arrived and lowered it as they departed. Clara discovered the practice when she came unexpectedly for the account book. I told her the exhibition was essential to patronage. She left the curtain cord in my hand and made no reply. It was a remarkably effective form of discourtesy.

### E22-003 | A Meal That Went Cold | 1870-01

Rosa carried a covered pot upstairs when the frost made the kitchen impossible. I was examining a little chamber whose pressure had scarcely altered in three hours. The cabbage smelled of cloves; its steam obscured the lower figures of my gauge. I directed her to leave it and remain silent. By the time I reached for the spoon, a gray skin had formed over the broth and a fly lay drowned at its edge. No meaningful change had occurred in the vessel. I ate standing, with the clock weight tapping the cupboard at every swing. Rosa's next delivery bore a scrap of paper: 'Prepared at eleven. Cold by one.' She continued this unnecessary chronological tyranny for years. I sometimes kept the slips in the blue ledger. They are not, of course, scientific records, though they have proved unusually accurate.

### E22-004 | The Room with No Witness | 1870-11

The first result was promising; the second, conducted without witnesses, more promising still. By the third trial I had written a sentence explaining what the next measurement ought to show. Lenz arrived to replace a spindle and I allowed him to remain. The needle trembled but did not rise. I struck the sentence from my notebook and accused the instrument of having been disturbed by his boots. He asked whether his boots had also disturbed the two earlier readings. It was a question designed by Providence to test my charitable temperament. We repeated the trial after supper with his boots beneath the table and my notebook left open to the original prediction. The result did not improve. He wrote 'unchanged' in the margin, slowly enough to be insulting. I permitted the word to remain. A record without an inconvenient witness is merely a diary of wishes.

### E22-005 | The Number after the Name | 1871-06

The young clerk insisted upon a number before he would release my parcel. It contained a series of stopped vessels, none of which would have interested him had they been made of diamonds. I supplied my surname and former appointment. He looked at the shelf, not at me, and repeated the number. His cuffs were wet from the morning rain. I imagined, briefly, taking him through the university halls and demonstrating the weight of the name he was so cheerfully disregarding. Instead I read the number from the receipt. The parcel had survived its journey intact. Thereafter I numbered my correspondence more carefully, sometimes replacing names with marks intelligible only to their recipients. This was prudent, if perhaps a little theatrical. The clerk, who never discovered either distinction, remained perfectly civil.

### E22-006 | A Crown without an Audience | 1871-12

I spent an afternoon arranging velvet threads about the Queen's chamber, then fashioned a dais of admirable proportion. She examined it once, touched the edge, and retired to an unpainted corner where a torn strip of paper bent away from the glass. I had left the paper there as an expedient. Its ungainly edge apparently served some purpose of hers. Twice I removed it, and twice she became still. At last I restored it without improving the workmanship. Under snowlight the royal apartment looked faintly absurd, a magnificent throne beside a shabby shelter. I showed visitors the throne. When alone, I watched the shelter. The difference between what I displayed and what she inhabited was not, I decided, a defect of the Queen. I could not find a more satisfactory theory of taste.

### E22-007 | The Printer's Unfinished Line | 1872-10

Mathilde had set the last paragraph when I discovered that the sentence I most admired belonged to a result not yet obtained. I wished to retain it as a statement of reasonable expectation. She asked whether I should call that reasonable expectation a conclusion when it appeared below my signature. The shop smelled of wet paper and discarded orange peel. The compositor had already distributed half the type for another commission. I removed the sentence. Mathilde charged me for the second setting without apology, though she allowed me to keep the spoiled proof. Years later I found it in the tower with the magnificent last line crossed out so heavily that the page was almost torn. I remembered the cost more readily than the embarrassment. That is either evidence of thrift or one of the deficiencies of autobiographical recollection.

### E22-008 | The Station Porter's Complaint | 1873-04

A porter with a scar on his chin declined to take my crate. He did not know what lay inside, but a sharp medicinal odor rose from one corner and the straw was damp. I assured him the material was harmless. He asked whether I would be willing to lift it onto his cart without gloves. I changed the subject to his company's inefficiencies and persuaded no one. Rosa happened to be collecting flour sacks. She furnished clean straw and a dry cloth, then waited while I repacked the vessels myself. The transaction cost half a day and an amount of pride not entered in the ledger. Thereafter I required double wrappings and allowed 'FRAGILE GLASS' to appear on some consignments. The porter's scar has remained in my mind more clearly than his name. I believe he was quite right about the crate, which is no reason to admire his manner.

### E22-009 | An Hour for a Sick Visitor | 1873-09

Emil came up the path without his customary certainty and sat before I had offered a chair. He said the hill had been steeper than usual. When he tried to examine my tables, the page shook in his fingers. I accused him of having arrived ill merely to escape admitting an error, then brought water in a jug with a crack down its side. Clara found him an hour later, ordered the window opened, and treated my objections as an atmospheric nuisance. I gave Emil the guest bed and covered the specimen table in the adjoining room. Two days passed without the intended demonstration. On leaving, he thanked me for the bed but said nothing of our disagreement. I was offended by this selective memory. I have nevertheless kept a serviceable mattress in that room ever since.

### E22-010 | The Bishop's Supper | 1874-02

The Archbishop had long seemed to regard the King's courtship as a public scandal. One winter evening, however, he remained still while the King approached the Queen, provided the meal had been moved away from the bright side of the vessel. I first interpreted this as an ecclesiastical conversion. It was an agreeable theory and quite without evidence. The bell of a distant chapel struck the hour, and the Archbishop turned toward the darker glass rather than toward either sovereign. I moved the bread again the following week and observed the same result. Whether he objected to romance, glare, or bad dining arrangements, I could not say. I continued describing him as disapproving to visitors; he was a better character that way. My private notes acquired a fourth column marked 'conditions of objection.' It was less entertaining and considerably more useful.

### E22-011 | The Chicken and the Ledger | 1874-12

I carried a roast chicken wrapped in paper to the crypt because the rain had driven me from the hill, and no one there required conversation. Georg Eberle discovered me at the stone ledge with a wing in my hand and the blue ledger open between two old inscriptions. He did not demand to know why I preferred this company. He pointed out that grease had seeped through the paper onto a relative's name. I apologized to the stone rather than to Georg, an economy of civility he did not appreciate, and scrubbed the mark with water from a bucket. I kept the resulting stain on the ledger page. I have dined in respectable houses where the dead were discussed with greater appetite and less decency. Georg later put a small folding stool beside the crypt door. He denied that it was for me.

### E22-012 | The Kingdom's Winter Account | 1875-05

I had drawn up a schedule by which the tower could subsist on reduced coal, fewer replacement seals, and a diet of nearly heroic simplicity. Rosa examined it and asked whether the jars would be consuming the same coal as the gentleman who had drawn the columns. Lenz found three joints in the heating pipes approaching failure. I accused them both of concerted extortion. They replied with estimates, delivered separately and therefore doubly irritating. I sold the rights to a harmless old lecture and spent the money on repairs. During the first hard frost the heating continued without interruption. I maintained that my economy had succeeded, since fewer vessels were broken. Rosa informed me that this was a novel definition of economy. She brought sweet tea without charging for it. I never discovered whether that omission was kindness or an error.

### E22-013 | A Disagreement over the Keys | 1876-03

I returned from an errand to find Clara in the upper room and the floor dry where a broken tap might have ruined a month's work. She had entered with Lenz's key, which I considered a trespass until she showed me the warped stopcock. I asked why she had not waited. She asked which of the drowned creatures she should have consulted for permission. Her coat smelled of wet wool and lamp soot. We spent the next hour drafting conditions under which she might enter in my absence, and I insisted on two signatures and an exact account of the keys. She signed without reading my most magnificent clauses. A second key hung afterward in her dispensary. I complained for weeks about the breach of scientific sovereignty. I also slept more easily on nights when the pipes knocked.

### E22-014 | The Unpublished Defeat | 1877-08

Mathilde appeared with a copied paragraph in her careful hand. I had written to a correspondent that a specimen's movement survived without a certain light source. My own ledger suggested that it had survived only when I failed to measure the light properly. I wished to annotate the ledger and consider the problem at leisure. Mathilde had already addressed an envelope. She informed me that the recipient possessed an incorrect claim with my name beneath it. I sent the correction, but refused her proposal for a printed notice that would have satisfied the most vindictive conscience. We argued over the sealing wax until the glass inkstand tipped and stained the tablecloth. The stain remains a livid blue. I recall it whenever someone praises the unqualified honesty of scientific men, an opinion ordinarily advanced by men who have not published results.

### E22-015 | The Case of Small Necessities | 1878-07

Marta Voss arrived with a botanical press, worn gloves, and questions so unromantic that I almost sent her away. She wished to know how often the water was changed, what the room smelled like before dawn, and whether the occupants behaved differently after a week of quiet. I had prepared an explanation of their extraordinary origins. She interrupted it to ask whether a damaged latch frightened them. We inspected the lower chambers first. Dried thyme from her satchel scented the room, masking the familiar sourness of the jars, and I had to admit that I had ceased noticing the latter. I gave her the maintenance book, not the secret notebook. The distinction seemed elegant. She returned it covered with marginal inquiries, every one about the stubborn business of keeping something alive. I answered them all, though not in the order she preferred.

### E22-016 | The Inventory Left Unfinished | 1878-12

Rosa spread flour sacks and invoices across the table while Voss examined the newest catalogue. Between them they had turned my pantry into an audit. I could account for every pane of glass, and nearly every spoonful of spirit used in preserving instruments. Voss asked where I had recorded the Queen's preference for the shabby paper corner. I said that preferences were not provisions. Rosa asked why I had purchased three yards of velvet if they were not. I began a separate page headed 'Unquantified Requirements' and hated it immediately. The ink blotted. I left the next line empty rather than pretend I could finish the category before supper. Years later, I found myself adding notes to the same page under different names. I have never enjoyed an inventory that records my ignorance so accurately.

## Materials That Would Not Become Answers (1879-1888)

### E23-001 | The Scent That Returned | 1879-03

Voss placed a sprig of thyme beside a cracked drain. A small inhabitant who had been motionless crossed the chamber in a sudden hurry. I called it an interest in botany. Voss reminded me that the same smell had been present when a vessel leaked the previous summer. I searched the old entries and found an account of frightened movement recorded as an effect of temperature. The room still smelled of wet plaster; verdigris showed beneath the copper basin. I had been so pleased with the neatness of my old explanation that I had failed to notice the neighboring detail. We restored the original arrangement without moving the cutting, then removed the cutting alone. The response changed. My notebook acquired the title 'Associations Not of My Choosing.' Voss borrowed it for a fortnight and returned it with more pages marked than I considered courteous.

### E23-002 | The Keeper of the False Relic | 1879-10

A gentleman offered me a splinter of bone in a silver mount, assuring me that it had moved beneath a medium's hand. I took it to Georg because he knew old cases and their ordinary dishonest repairs. He turned the mount over and recognized the velvet beneath it as a strip his father had sold three summers earlier. The bone may have been venerable; the stage upon which it performed was not. I purchased the affair for less than its owner expected and placed it in a cabinet beside a broken clock and a portrait whose eyes appeared to follow visitors. The gentlemen who inspected these objects invariably chose the relic as the most impressive. I allowed the deception to proceed for a minute before showing the stitching. This was not kindness. Their faces became much more interesting after the revelation.

### E23-003 | An Ugly Room for a Lovely Specimen | 1880-04

The north room had limewash falling from one corner in curls like dead petals. I intended to remake it in white tile and bright metal, having recently found a specimen of exquisite form whose habits were irregular. Clara moved the chamber away from its polished mirror while the men repaired the shutters. For two weeks the creature slept consistently. When we restored the mirror, it woke and remained awake. A man more devoted to simple conclusions might have inferred an aversion to reflections. I was devoted to finding an explanation that would preserve the mirror, which had cost a great deal. Eventually I covered it with a tin sheet. Clara kept the renovation estimate folded in the account book. I could tell she enjoyed the fact that an ugly room had improved the experiment. I gave her no such satisfaction openly.

### E23-004 | The Tale Told at a Proper Table | 1880-11

Mathilde served boiled fish and a plum preserve so sharp that it punished the tongue before one could admire it. Voss asked what had become of the handsome specimen I had displayed in a drawing. I offered an account of its promising proportions. She asked a second time. The fish cooled. I admitted it had not survived a modest alteration I had made in pursuit of further symmetry. No one expressed the horror I had prepared to rebuke. Mathilde asked how many attempts I had made afterward. The number was disagreeable. I gave it anyway, and conversation improved. Thereafter those two ladies developed the habit of asking for 'the other version' of any report I presented too elegantly. Their impertinence has preserved several useful facts, while rendering dinner a less restful institution than I had imagined.

### E23-005 | A Sunday without Results | 1881-05

Rosa stopped me on the river path with a basket of pears and a package too heavy for a woman who refused to admit that her knee troubled her. I said I was bound home to record a specimen's afternoon movements. She asked whether the specimen had agreed to an appointment. I carried the package to her sister's door. We ate a pear apiece on the return journey, the skins bitter beneath the sweetness, while bees worked the bank and my shoes accumulated mud. We spoke of a neighbor's roof, an unpromising horse, and the increasing cost of lamp oil. I returned after sunset. Nothing had happened in the chamber that could not be observed the following day. I resisted the temptation to call the excursion restorative. It was merely time spent living in a world that declined to announce results at hourly intervals.

### E23-006 | The Lever That Remembered | 1881-12

The brass lever had once responded cleanly to the slightest pressure. Now it caught at an angle and stained the thumb with grease. Lenz proposed a new fitting. I rejected it for want of funds, then discovered that the worn motion repeated more faithfully than the newer apparatus beside it. We measured the deviation and marked the handle with a black line. After that I could feel the small resistance through the glove before reading the scale. The instrument had acquired a character. Lenz called this the ordinary life of machinery. I objected to such undisciplined personification while returning to the same handle each morning. When the leather grip finally split, I had him replace the leather and preserve the metal. He shook his head and did exactly as I requested, which was, I believe, the first time he obeyed an instruction without argument.

### E23-007 | A Lesson in the Spoiled Plate | 1882-06

A plate that should have remained clear acquired a milky bloom by morning. I was prepared to interpret it as an unanticipated phase of growth. Emil sniffed the rim and asked when the window had last been opened. The room was full of summer flies. I had omitted the cover for a moment while adjusting a lamp. It would have been a simple matter to photograph the bloom and discuss it as evidence that my formula was unusually vigorous. Instead I labeled it 'intrusion suspected' and placed it beside the next clean trial. A visitor later admired the clouded plate above all the others. I let him admire it until he had finished talking. The pleasure I took in the ensuing correction was perhaps not scientific. Emil remarked that I had, for once, resisted a magnificent error. He was unwise enough to add 'for once.'

### E23-008 | The Letter Sent to the Wrong Address | 1882-12

A package returned with my invented surname struck through and 'unknown' stamped beneath it. I had devised the address to protect the laboratory from journalists and creditors. Unfortunately a correspondent whose instruments I respected had enclosed three pages of observations and declined to risk a second dispatch. Mathilde arranged a forwarding place through her shop, requiring me to sign a written authorization in my proper name. I felt the loss of an elegant disguise more keenly than the arrival of the pages. The data contradicted an assumption I had defended at some expense. I worked through the figures by candlelight while Mathilde sorted invoices downstairs. On returning the pages I thanked the correspondent for his diligence but not Mathilde for hers. She charged me for forwarding; I paid at once, which was as near gratitude as the circumstances required.

### E23-009 | The Winter Memory in Warm Weather | 1883-05

The day was hot enough to soften the leather binding of my notebook. The Queen withdrew from her chamber's exposed side as she had during the bitter winter two years before. I announced a hereditary disposition toward melancholy. Voss examined what had changed and found a folded cloth I had used to insulate the glass during that earlier season. We removed it. The Queen moved toward the light again. This did not prove she remembered a winter; it proved my grand theory of seasonal temperament was an unreliable substitute for looking beneath the jar. We repeated the trial after supper with the cloth arranged differently. I drank a little pear brandy and made notes that became less elegant as the evening wore on. Voss wrote the temperatures in her own hand, an affront I had invited by misplacing the thermometer twice.

### E23-010 | A Visitor Who Wanted Certainty | 1883-11

An instrument student at the Munich reading room studied the section drawing beside my elbow and asked whether the side joint was visible from the angle I had chosen. I told him that the drawing described a principle, not a carpenter's bill. He offered to turn the book so we might inspect its plates together. He smelled faintly of lamp oil and the leather used in repairing instruments. We found a missing dimension, but I refused to acknowledge that it invalidated the elegance of the figure. He copied it for me on a slip I folded and put away without asking his surname. Later I attempted to identify the stranger by his annotations and discovered that I had never asked his name. Memory is capable of excellent stage management; it reliably makes the people one disregarded seem more important in retrospect.

### E23-011 | The Clockmaker's Dinner | 1884-06

Novak had promised to inspect the travelling mechanism at the inn. He arrived late, limping, and spoke of his brother's long illness while the soup congealed between us. Voss listened. I checked the time twice and could not resist opening the instrument case. Novak closed it without raising his voice. We spent an hour discussing doctors, rail connections, and the indignity of standing in a corridor for news. Only then did he examine the gears. He worked in silence, his fingers black with oil, and corrected an error I had been concealing behind an elaborate adjustment. The following morning the mechanism kept time better than before. I have never established whether the improvement depended upon my patient listening. It is an experiment I shall not repeat deliberately. His brother recovered somewhat, which I learned from a postcard months later.

### E23-012 | The Sale of an Unlovely Drawing | 1884-12

The patron selected the finest drawing, naturally, a specimen with a serene face and impossible hands. Mathilde pointed to a rough plate showing the same experiment before I had improved the proportions on paper. It bore a stain, three pencilled corrections, and an honest note that the subject had not survived the final interval. She proposed printing both. I accused her of conducting a campaign against beauty, a charge she did not deny. The pamphlet appeared with the damaged plate facing the ideal one. Two letters came asking about the stain, for it obscured an important measurement. Nobody inquired about the serene face. For a week I claimed the printing was an outrageous concession to vulgar tastes. I then ordered more copies. A flawed image had performed one of science's few truly decent offices: it had annoyed the right people into asking questions.

### E23-013 | The Restorer's Second Visit | 1885-11

Julian Adler came to repair a delicate catch whose failure I had attributed to careless workmanship. He showed me the wear had begun on my side of the apparatus, where a misaligned weight dragged upon the fitting. I disputed the matter for nearly an hour. He did not rise to leave, nor flatter me by yielding. Instead he placed the removed part beneath the lamp and invited me to watch it catch at precisely the point I had insisted was sound. When he had finished, I found another instrument requiring inspection. Its fault was trifling. He saw that at once and repaired it anyway. I do not pretend this was an experiment in companionship. It was merely that his departure would have interrupted an argument in which I had not yet achieved a satisfactory conclusion. He came back the following month without an appointment.

### E23-014 | The Medium's Wrong Name | 1886-08

At the salon a medium pronounced a name I had written on a folded card which no one, I believed, had opened. She misplaced one letter. An error of that sort ought to have disposed of the claim. Instead it unsettled me, for it was the error I made myself when writing the name hurriedly. The room smelled of hot velvet and smoking lamp wicks. The hostess held the cards while the company congratulated itself upon a revelation. I asked to see the original. It had vanished amid the customary confusion of chairs and sleeves. Voss advised recording precisely what had happened rather than announcing that we had witnessed proof or deception. I drafted both announcements before tearing them up. The name survives in my notebook with its wrong letter circled. I still cannot say whether I had written it incorrectly at the start.

### E23-015 | The Afternoon that Did Not Belong to Me | 1887-05

Julian had invited me to his lodging with the understanding that I would bring no work. I smuggled a folded sketch inside my coat. He saw its corner before I had removed my gloves and took it from me without ceremony. He placed it beneath a coffee cup, where a ring of dark moisture spread across the paper. I almost objected. Instead we opened the window and listened to carts passing on wet cobbles. The blanket over his chair smelled of clean wool; his coffee had grown a thin skin, which he stirred back into the cup with unconcern. We spoke of his childhood aunt, whose portrait he disliked, and of the journey I had declined the previous autumn. I went away with no new result in the ledger. The stained sketch remained at his lodging. On my next visit I brought nothing at all and found the precaution strangely burdensome.

### E23-016 | The Instrument No One Could Inherit | 1888-10

After the funeral I found an old instrument hanging above the bench, its control handle worn smooth in a pattern made by a hand I would no longer encounter. The spring had become brittle. Lenz proposed replacing it and leaving the original in a box. I forbade the disassembly. Several days later Clara asked whether I intended to use the apparatus or worship it. She was being deliberately offensive. I had Lenz replace the spring and leave the handle intact. The first movement came with a click slightly different from the one I remembered. I disliked it. Nevertheless the readings were sound, and the restored instrument remained in service. When alone I sometimes put my thumb on the polished place where the earlier hand had rested. I did not enter this habit in the laboratory book.

## The Thirty-Year Table (1889-1898)

### E24-001 | The Familiar Invoice | 1889-04

Rosa set her battered tin measure on the table and announced that her knee would no longer permit the hill after heavy snow. She had brought a younger cousin to manage the cart. I protested the substitution as though a coal delivery were a university appointment. Then she produced her notebook. It held thirty years of weights, prices, missing payments and remarks in an irritable handwriting remarkably like my own. Beside one winter she had written 'Professor forgot to eat' and beside another 'Professor swore the Queen preferred the dark.' She knew more of the household's history than any published memoir. I spent an evening comparing her figures with mine and found that my accounts did not balance in two exceptionally expensive years. Rosa pointed to the repairs I had charged to the specimens instead of to myself. I paid what I owed her. I left her descriptions uncorrected.

### E24-002 | The Keeper's Revised Paper | 1889-12

Clara arrived carrying brown parcels and a clear account of the morning's work. I gave her a page of instructions, arranged so precisely that any intelligent person ought to have been grateful. She asked why the north shutter must be closed before the last bell, why one chamber remained partly shaded, and whether she was forbidden to enter the upper room because of danger or vanity. My explanations became less satisfactory as the questions proceeded. I gave her the first three; the fourth concerned my private formulas and remained my affair. She wrote 'unexplained restriction' beside it. I did not dismiss her. By the following week she had made a small list of procedures that no longer served their stated purpose. I concealed my annoyance beneath the observation that a keeper with a mind of her own was a more expensive form of apparatus. She replied by requesting better paper.

### E24-003 | The Metronome's Empty Beat | 1890-07

The Ballerina responded, as she had always done, to the phrase she preferred. I called it exquisite discrimination and played the passage again. Clara asked whether the creature stirred before the first note. I accused her of mistaking the movement of my hand for the movement inside the glass. She placed a felt pad beneath the mechanism and counted the beats aloud until I could bear the sound no longer. Then she stopped one beat before the accustomed phrase. The Ballerina moved. The room carried the stale fragrance of rosewater used by the former keeper; the little hammer hung above a note it never struck. I spent a fortnight examining whether anticipation survived when the familiar sequence was interrupted. It did sometimes, and sometimes did not. I have not found a more economical explanation than learning. I still play the whole passage when visitors come.

### E24-004 | An Anniversary Not My Own | 1890-12

Mathilde's dining room was full of paper flowers and an absurdly large cinnamon cake. I had brought a manuscript in a leather case, believing this to be the subject of her note. The gathering celebrated her mother's seventieth year. No one asked about my papers. I sat near the shutters, drank weak tea, and endured a child's account of a mechanical toy whose wheels would not remain attached. When the child asked my opinion, I examined the axle and suggested a repair. Mathilde took the case from beneath my chair and placed it by the door, where it remained all evening. I objected when I left, saying that she might at least have allowed me a glance at the proofs. She reminded me that she had invited a man, not a manuscript. I remember the cake rather well. I do not remember what I had intended to argue.

### E24-005 | The Second Hand on the Page | 1891-08

A summer storm darkened the window while I recorded the Queen's refusal of a freshly decorated compartment. I described the refusal as sovereign disdain. Anna had watched from the other side and said the Queen entered the room twice before retreating after a flash of lightning. Voss, who arrived later, believed that the creature avoided a certain smell from the new varnish. All three interpretations were plausible. I wanted my conclusion, because it was the most becoming to its subject and creator alike. Instead we wrote separate accounts in violet ink, signing the margins. I warned them that multiplying witnesses did not necessarily multiply truth. Anna answered that my single witness had hardly done better. The notebook thickened with such competing voices over the next years. I disliked its appearance, but increasingly feared the neat books that lacked them.

### E24-006 | A Gift Refused for the Right Reason | 1891-12

I presented Julian with an instrument restored so perfectly that even its former owner might have failed to recognize it. He admired the work and asked whether I expected him to take over the measurements I had been neglecting. I said I expected nothing. He opened the leather case and showed me the list of trials I had enclosed beneath the lid. Frost formed on his window while the lamp smoked. The silence that followed was one of those rare intervals during which my wit deserted me through no fault of the company. He closed the case and returned it. I carried the instrument home, furious with the ingratitude of a man who had apparently understood my intention too well. We exchanged ordinary letters afterward, without a ceremonial reconciliation. I still possess the unused instrument. Its workmanship remains flawless, a fact not entirely to its credit.

### E24-007 | The Supper in the Wrong Room | 1892-03

I had grown accustomed to eating wherever the day's work released me: at the bench, in the crypt, once upon a crate of empty jars. Clara objected to my chicken in the upper room not because the place was unclean, but because I had forgotten that she and Anna were waiting below. I insisted that an important observation had detained me. The observation had been the King watching his reflection. She offered no counterargument and brought the food upstairs. The skin had cooled to a disagreeable firmness. We ate at the library table with candlewax collecting upon an old botanical catalogue. I declared a weekly meal with witnesses unnecessary. They continued it without seeking another declaration. There were evenings when I arranged my experiments to finish early. I have never been able to explain that scheduling choice as a scientific necessity.

### E24-008 | The Glass that Could Not Grow | 1892-11

I had enlarged the chamber with extraordinary attention to proportion. Its supports were stronger, its seals finer, and its occupant no nearer to remaining viable beyond the familiar interval. The fourth failure was accompanied by a shattered rim that cut Lenz's finger. He wrapped it in clean linen and went home without waiting for my apology. Voss said the next attempt would reproduce the same ignorance at a greater cost. I replied that the scale of an error is sometimes what reveals its nature. She asked what new variable I proposed to change. I could name none. We suspended the series. For weeks I passed the large empty vessel and felt the peculiar insult of an instrument that had performed all its duties while the hypothesis collapsed. I did not dismantle it. A large failure occupies a room with much greater authority than a small one.

### E24-009 | The Printed Marriage Notice | 1893-05

I came to the shop for proofs and saw a marriage announcement in the newspaper stacked for wrapping. It concerned a man I had once known rather better than the world would have approved, now described in three lines as a respectable husband. A crushed carnation lay on the counter and marked the paper with a thin red stain. Mathilde pretended to be absorbed in an invoice. I cut out the notice with her paper knife, folded it twice, and put it into my pocket. She said nothing and brought the proofs. I corrected every comma with unusual severity. That evening I filed the clipping between measurements of vessel pressure where no rational archivist would think to look for it. It has no scientific value. I have retained things of less value under that pretext for much longer.

### E24-010 | An Honest Catalogue of Imperfections | 1893-12

I wished to prepare a catalogue of the little court suitable for a visitor who had offered to purchase drawings. The Queen's profile deserved first place, followed by the Ballerina and the King. Anna placed her own list beside mine. Her headings were not beauty, proportion, and dignity but movement, tolerance of cold, appetite, and response to unfamiliar objects. The Devil, whom I had assigned an uncertain rank, led in two practical categories. I accused Anna of preparing an inventory of kitchen servants. She accused me of preparing a catalogue of porcelain. We retained both. A visiting collector favored my list and pronounced himself delighted. I found myself examining Anna's afterward. It had the intolerable merit of explaining why several apparently ungainly subjects survived difficulties that defeated their more finished companions.

### E24-011 | The Dreams I Could Not Manufacture | 1894-07

The glass door had appeared in my sleep often enough that I began to resent its independence. I arranged the same lamp, locked the same iron latch, and placed a scrap of frosted glass upon the bedside table. Nothing followed except an uncomfortable night and the smell of wet linen after the morning rain. I repeated the arrangement for a week. On the sixth morning I wrote down a fragment of a dream involving a railway ticket and an unknown child, neither of which suited my experiment. Voss asked whether I had considered allowing the dream to occur without permission. I accused her of proposing a research method fit for poets. I abandoned the apparatus. Months afterward the glass door returned in sleep, but I could not remember whether it opened. I suspect my irritation improved the memory less than I wished.

### E24-012 | A Visitor Who Wanted the Recipe | 1895-06

Otto Wernig arrived carrying an old treatise wrapped in dry leather. He expected me to acknowledge a debt to its author. I was inclined to deny any resemblance between ancient recipes and the actual discipline of my work. Yet the marginal drawing he showed me resembled one of my discarded chamber designs closely enough to spoil the afternoon. We spent hours comparing vocabulary, separating what an alchemist might have meant by seed from what I had observed in matter. He asked where the resemblance ended. I told him that a bibliography does not entitle a bookseller to a formula. He accepted the distinction and requested a cigar. I refused to surrender my last good one, which proved to be a greater offense. He left with a catalogue of works to find. I retained the treatise and entered his name beside a debt, not a revelation.

### E24-013 | A Refusal That Cost Something | 1895-12

Julian had arranged an excursion in winter, a week away from instruments and the incessant improvisations by which I kept my household running. I had agreed months before. At the station café I announced that the Queen required observation during an uncertain transition. Julian reminded me that I had already appointed Anna to observe her. Outside, steam drifted beneath the lamps and wet passengers shook rain from their coats. He peeled an orange with the care he gave to repairing a delicate hinge. He asked whether I had simply decided not to come. I answered with a discussion of risk that would have persuaded any reasonable colleague. He finished the orange, put on a dry glove, and said he would make the journey alone. I watched his train depart. The experiment proceeded without my presence for part of the following day. It remained inconclusive.

### E24-014 | The Negative in the Drawer | 1896-10

The darkroom smelled of developer, damp cardboard, and the old wood of its drawers. I had been prepared to reveal an internal architecture of such elegance that certain metaphysical objections would at last be deprived of their theater. The negative showed a pale region whose boundary shifted when the subject and apparatus were arranged again. Voss asked why a universal organ should change position with the angle of the lamp. I gave a long account of imperfect technique, all of which was true and none of which rescued the claim. Anna labeled the plate with the failed settings and filed it under method rather than discovery. A year later she used it to warn a visitor against drawing conclusions from a beautiful photograph. I objected to the word beautiful. The visitor admired it anyway. I still dislike the drawer in which it rests.

### E24-015 | The Lesson Received by the Devil | 1897-04

The Devil looked splendid in his little black cape, a figure made for defying expectations, which ought to have warned me against requiring submission. I had arranged a simple demonstration for two visitors. He was to cross the platform when I tapped a chipped saucer. He remained still. I tapped again, rather harder. Anna moved to the opposite side of the chamber and he approached her at once. The guests laughed. I assured them that the arrangement had tested attraction to familiar caretakers, though such had not been my purpose. After their departure I repeated the situation without an audience and obtained results sufficiently variable to forbid easy interpretation. I stopped demonstrating obedience. It was an ugly decision, since the little subject's refusal had become the most popular feature of my collection. Perhaps his judgment of the performance was better than mine.

### E24-016 | The Room That Needed No Curtain | 1898-11

By the last autumn before the news from Henry, the western curtain had acquired a seam where I had repaired it years earlier. I stood beside Clara while Anna cleaned the window, and the Queen moved behind a little paper shelter I had never successfully improved. The chamber required new glass, new seals, and an accountant willing to forgive our figures. What it did not require, at least that evening, was another audience. I left the curtain down until the lamps were lit and forbade visitors during the following hour. Clara asked whether I had become tender. I informed her that interruption affected repeatability. She accepted the explanation with a smile so small it was barely an observable event. I can still summon the sound of the glass being cleaned. I cannot say why that ordinary noise has survived in memory when so many grander apparatuses have vanished.
