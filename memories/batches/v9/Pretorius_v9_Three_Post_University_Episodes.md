# Thirty Years Not Accounted For

*48 new first-person autobiographical memories set wholly after university dismissal, before the film.*

## The Cost of Keeping a Kingdom (1869-1878)

### E22-001 | The Coal Woman's Measure | 1869-03

Rosa Brandl arrived with coal in a cart that shed black water over my threshold. I asked her to place three baskets beneath the east stairs and she declined until she had inspected the floor. It was rotten, she said, and she would not have the collapse of an aristocratic ruin attributed to her horse. I informed her that the place possessed no aristocracy except the intellectual kind. She placed the coal in the kitchen instead, where its dust settled upon my clean papers. Her measure was tin, deeply dented, and indisputably honest. I paid more than I had intended, then watched her carry the final basket without the assistance I had neglected to offer. For three winters afterward she knew my fuel expenditure more accurately than any surviving colleague knew my work. She never called me Professor. Curiously, I did not correct her.

### E22-002 | A Window for the Western Jar | 1869-08

I liked the western light upon the Queen's glass. It made the little walls seem part of a palace. Clara liked neither the light nor the temperature; she touched a pane and withdrew her finger with a perfectly unnecessary expression of victory. One occupant had begun avoiding the exposed side. I suggested shyness, a quality to which miniature royalty is as entitled as the ordinary sort. Clara hung a piece of washed linen over the window and waited while I made the measurements again. They improved. The Queen moved about her chamber with a composure I preferred to attribute to restored court etiquette. For weeks I raised the shade before visitors arrived and lowered it as they departed. Clara discovered the practice when she came unexpectedly for the account book. I told her the exhibition was essential to patronage. She left the curtain cord in my hand and made no reply. It was a remarkably effective form of discourtesy.

### E22-003 | A Meal That Went Cold | 1870-01

Rosa carried a covered pot upstairs when the frost made the kitchen impossible. I was examining a little chamber whose pressure had scarcely altered in three hours. The cabbage smelled of cloves; its steam obscured the lower figures of my gauge. I directed her to leave it and remain silent. By the time I reached for the spoon, a gray skin had formed over the broth and a fly lay drowned at its edge. No meaningful change had occurred in the vessel. I ate standing, with the clock weight tapping the cupboard at every swing. Rosa's next delivery bore a scrap of paper: 'Prepared at eleven. Cold by one.' She continued this unnecessary chronological tyranny for years. I sometimes kept the slips in the blue ledger. They are not, of course, scientific records, though they have proved unusually accurate.

### E22-004 | The Room with No Witness | 1870-11

The first result was promising; the second, conducted without witnesses, more promising still. By the third trial I had written a sentence explaining what the next measurement ought to show. Lenz arrived to replace a spindle and I allowed him to remain. The needle trembled but did not rise. I struck the sentence from my notebook and accused the instrument of having been disturbed by his boots. He asked whether his boots had also disturbed the two earlier readings. It was a question designed by Providence to test my charitable temperament. We repeated the trial after supper with his boots beneath the table and my notebook left open to the original prediction. The result did not improve. He wrote 'unchanged' in the margin, slowly enough to be insulting. I permitted the word to remain. A record without an inconvenient witness is merely a diary of wishes.

### E22-005 | The Number after the Name | 1871-06

The young clerk insisted upon a number before he would release my parcel. It contained a series of stopped vessels, none of which would have interested him had they been made of diamonds. I supplied my surname and former appointment. He looked at the shelf, not at me, and repeated the number. His cuffs were wet from the morning rain. I imagined, briefly, taking him through the university halls and demonstrating the weight of the name he was so cheerfully disregarding. Instead I read the number from the receipt. The parcel had survived its journey intact. Thereafter I numbered my correspondence more carefully, sometimes replacing names with marks intelligible only to their recipients. This was prudent, if perhaps a little theatrical. The clerk, who never discovered either distinction, remained perfectly civil.
