# Complete chronological autobiography

## 1859

### E09-001 | The Magistrate's Waiting Room | 1859-09

My father permitted me to sit in his antechamber while he disposed of a quarrel about a millstream. I had brought a notebook on respiration and had every intention of keeping my own company. A miller's widow, Greta Falk, waited opposite me, turning the seam of a glove inside out with her thumb. She possessed a drawing of the watercourse; the man who disputed her claim had arrived with a solicitor and a parcel tied in green tape. My father examined both accounts, asked why one measurement had been taken after rain, and sent them away to obtain a third. I called this an abdication of judgment. He said a magistrate who rushed to pronounce upon two insufficient accounts was not brave but idle. I was irritated to discover that he had listened more closely than I. I still remember Mrs. Falk's glove. I remember less about the millstream, though its particulars were the subject of my lecture at supper.

### E09-002 | The Ink That Wasn't Mine | 1859-12

Mrs. Falk sent a note thanking my father for the measurement that settled her boundary dispute. He gave it to me to file, and I read the sentence in which she attributed the useful question to him. I had, of course, objected first to the original figures, though my objection had been substantially wrong. I carried the letter upstairs and began composing a correction. My father saw the draft when he came for his spectacles. He observed that I had been present and that the examination had occurred, but that I had contributed very little to its success. He did not forbid the letter. He set a fresh sheet beside me, so that I might send it legibly. I burnt the correction in the stove. At breakfast I described the business as unimportant, and he made no reply. For weeks afterward I was excessively precise about the household accounts, as though Mrs. Falk might someday ask me to calculate another boundary.

## 1860

### E01-001 | The Empty Specimen Drawer | 1860-10

On my third day at the university, I was given a drawer that smelled of camphor and contained nothing except a dead beetle pinned through its thorax. Herr Kappel, keeper of the cabinet, called it an accommodation. I called it a warning about academic generosity. He had lost the key to the better drawer, or claimed to have lost it, and made me sign twice for a cracked magnifying lens. I spent the afternoon arranging the beetle's wings under the glass, discovering that I could change the appearance of movement merely by altering the angle of the light. When Kappel returned, the drawer was cleaner than he had ever kept it. The following morning a second key appeared beside my ink bottle. He never admitted leaving it. I never thanked him in public.

### E09-003 | A Question Lost in the Gallery | 1860-11

The back benches were crowded, and a visiting lecturer spoke for forty minutes on an animal's supposed obedience to an applied current. I had prepared an objection about the interval between stimulus and motion. I delivered it from the gallery in what I believed was an adequate voice. Nobody answered. Reuter told me afterward that the lecturer could not hear a word over the stove. I repeated the question the following week, standing where the lamp threw my shadow across the demonstration table. The lecturer heard it and disliked it, which was a considerable improvement. Heller, watching from the aisle, remarked that I had altered neither the data nor the argument, merely its delivery. I said an idea has little obligation to be modest when the room is hard of hearing. Ever since, I have preferred to choose my position before speaking. One must not confuse this with a fondness for attention. Attention is the door through which evidence must enter.

### E01-002 | The Candle at the Stair | 1860-12

Professor Heller's final lecture ended in darkness because the caretaker had economized on candles. The students stamped about and made a nuisance of themselves, while I remained to question a diagram in which the nervous system had been represented as a line of telegraph wire. Heller relit one candle and invited me to prove the analogy wrong. I could not. What I could prove was that his diagram omitted interruption, delay and recovery. He listened until the wax had almost reached his fingers, then informed me that objections become useful only when their author can name a measurement. I went home irritated enough to fill six pages. Years later I could still recall the smell of his singed cuff. I have never forgiven him for giving me good advice in such a disagreeable manner.

## 1861

### E09-004 | The Two-Minute Objection | 1861-01

Heller offered me two minutes at the end of a practical session to show why a neat table of reactions concealed a variable interval. I had brought eight pages. When the bell rang he closed the notebook on my fingers and said I had been allotted two minutes, not immortality. The students laughed. Reuter helped me reduce the argument to a single sketch and three measurements, although I accused him of reducing a cathedral to a doorway. At the next session I presented the shorter case. Heller kept the sketch. A month later he used it in his teaching without remembering, at least in public, whose hand had drawn it. This was my first practical education in the distinction between being heard and being credited. I became economical when I needed a door opened, extravagant when I wished to ensure no one could later mistake the man who had entered.

### E01-003 | The Glassblower's Apprentice | 1861-02

Jakob Lenz was eighteen and had already burned two shirts practicing joints that would not leak. He regarded my sketch of a divided vessel, scratched on the back of an examination notice, as an insult to glass. The neck, he said, would break when warm. I told him that I had calculated the pressure. He heated a waste tube and it broke in precisely the place he had marked. I purchased the ruined piece and kept it on my desk, partly as a reminder and partly because he expected me to hide it. We spent the next month learning to disagree over a workbench without raising our voices. He called me Doctor long before I had the right to the title. I suspect he enjoyed my corrections.

### E13-001 | The Chair at the Back of the Hall | 1861-03

I came to the municipal hall for a lecture on acoustics and found a woman in shirtsleeves striking the floor with the butt of a broom. Each blow summoned a dull complaint from beneath the boards. She was testing the platform before the evening's recital. I offered a more refined method of locating the loose joists, which she rejected because she had already found them. Her name was Leonie Rausch. She repaired costumes, altered scenery, and regarded scholars as men who expected someone else to fetch the ladder. I watched her wedge a scrap of wood beneath the offending plank, then clap at the rear wall to hear how the room returned the sound. When I asked whether she knew anything of Helmholtz, she asked whether Helmholtz had ever tried to make a tenor audible over an audience eating nuts. I went home irritated and borrowed a book on the physiology of hearing. The next week I returned with measurements. She had repaired the platform in the meantime. The book was not wasted, although my visit appeared to be.

### E01-004 | A Disputed Frog | 1861-04

Emil Reuter said a twitch proved an animal had obeyed the experimenter. Heller had asked us to distinguish response from recovery; Emil preferred a demonstration that drew applause. I had watched the frog's leg move even when the stimulus was applied by a boy whose hands were shaking. The animal, I remarked, was not consulting anyone's intentions. Emil laughed, and I laughed too, though at rather different things. Afterward I found him behind the lecture hall replacing the soiled bandage on his thumb. He had been burned preparing the apparatus and had concealed it to avoid losing his place. I helped tie the clean cloth. The next week he lent me his German translation of a French physiology paper, still stained at the corner with iodine.

### E09-005 | The Demonstrator's Courtesy | 1861-05

A demonstrator repeated my objection about the delayed response of tissue as though it had occurred to him in his bath. I waited until after his audience dispersed to ask whether he remembered our earlier conversation. He remembered it perfectly and declared that ideas belonged to the discipline once spoken aloud. Reuter suggested the fellow may have arrived at the same conclusion independently. I replied that coincidence had acquired remarkably accurate handwriting. For a fortnight I dated every loose scrap and initialled the backs of diagrams. Heller saw the practice and asked whether I intended to preserve the birth certificate of every thought. I answered that I had not yet received the privileges of an established mind and must provide my own witnesses. The initials later proved useful in an entirely different disagreement. I have never found it necessary to explain to the demonstrator why I ceased lending him notebooks.

### E16-001 | The Other Door of the Theatre | 1861-05

Leonie led me through the theatre by the door ordinarily used for scenery, not by the splendid entrance at which the patrons congratulated themselves for attending art. A young draughtsman was measuring the side flats with a carpenter's rule. Theodor Vahl had ink on his cuff and a way of listening which made my more elaborate remarks sound, for once, like a waste of the listener's time. When I said the false perspective was wrong, he asked for the distance from which I intended to view it. We spent the evening moving a lamp by inches and watching painted columns become convincing. His fingers were stained blue from the pigments; mine from the laboratory. He proposed that we exchange notebooks, an offer sufficiently improper to interest me. Leonie warned that he kept drawings better than he kept appointments. On my departure Theodor handed me a sketch of the lamp's position. He had put my name under the diagram, an unnecessary courtesy which I nevertheless examined twice before filing it. I returned to the theatre three evenings later to correct a measurement I had already checked.

### E01-005 | The Woman at the Dispensary | 1861-06

Clara Weiss knew a spoiled tincture by its look and deeply resented gentlemen who called that knowledge intuition. She set a jar before me, its yellow sediment suspended like a dirty sunset, and asked for a diagnosis. I supplied one at admirable length. She removed the stopper. The odor disposed of my theory in a second. Her late husband's formula books were full of mistakes, she said, and she had corrected them while the learned men who purchased his medicines were busy praising his genius. I returned with a written amendment. She read it, said my handwriting was scarcely an improvement upon my nose, and sold me filter paper at full price. I came back the following week. No one at the university charged so honestly for contradiction.

### E09-006 | A Joke Received as an Answer | 1861-08

Clara found me in the dispensary arguing with Reuter about the difference between an unsuccessful experiment and an incompetent experimenter. She asked whether either of us could distinguish the two before her midday customers arrived. I replied that the incompetent man usually spoke longest. Reuter laughed; Clara did not. A woman waiting with a prescription asked if we were discussing her physician. I could have made an explanation, but instead asked to see the prescribed dose and found it needlessly ambiguous. Clara corrected the label and served the woman. Only later did I discover she had kept my joke and repeated it to her apprentices when they began quarrelling instead of working. She did not credit me, nor did I ask her to. My amusement had done a small service which argument alone could not. There is no dignified scientific account of why I enjoyed that discovery.

### E01-006 | The Locked Shelf of Errors | 1861-09

Kappel showed me a neglected shelf of theses that had been withdrawn or rejected. They were tied with green tape and had the dusty dignity of small coffins. I asked why he had preserved them when the faculty had ordered their titles removed from the catalogue. He replied that a catalogue and a collection served different masters. One manuscript described a failed attempt to preserve a salamander's tissues during winter; its author had mistaken cold for death. I spent a week checking its observations instead of mocking them. Kappel produced more green tape when I returned the pages in order. I understood his bargain: he would trust me with condemned ideas provided I treated their authors as people who might have noticed something.

### E13-002 | The Gesture That Saved a Lecture | 1861-10

The lecture apparatus arrived with its glass neck broken, and Heller informed me that the evening would proceed without the demonstration. He meant to make the absence a quiet rebuke. Leonie, who had come to return a borrowed lens, noticed that the audience could not see what I had arranged behind the lectern even if the instrument had survived. She placed an empty bell jar beneath the lamp, brought two chairs forward, and instructed me to stand between them while I described the experiment. I thought the arrangement vulgar. Then I watched the listeners lean forward. At one point I extended my fingers over the empty jar to indicate a boundary, and the room became extraordinarily still. I had not supplied a particle of new evidence. Nevertheless every sentence had acquired weight. Heller congratulated himself on the success of an event he had nearly cancelled. Leonie requested the return of her lens before he could claim that too. Since that evening I have taken care to consider where I stand when making an important assertion. Purely for purposes of audibility, naturally.

### E01-007 | The Cold Lecture Bench | 1861-11

We were obliged to count reactions in an unheated room with our pens stiffening between fingers. Emil complained of the cold and misread two intervals; I complained of Emil and misread a third. We warmed our hands around a kettle meant for the caretaker and repeated the observations. The figures agreed less closely with our preferred theory than the first set had done. I proposed omitting the earlier series. Emil looked at me for a long moment and said that if he had suggested such a thing I should have enjoyed humiliating him. He was correct, which made his tone intolerable. I entered both series, labelling the conditions with unusual diligence. We drank the caretaker's tea and replaced it the following morning.

### E16-002 | A Walk That Was Not an Experiment | 1861-11

Theodor asked me to walk with him after the last performance. I assumed he wished to discuss the problem of reflected light, for which I had brought notes. Instead he bought two paper cones of roasted chestnuts and directed us toward the river. The air smelled of wet stone and spent coal. We spoke first of the new bridge, then of his mother, who believed a draughtsman should seek employment more regular than a theatre. I offered an admirably detailed argument against maternal interference. He laughed and said that I had mistaken a complaint for a question. We reached the bend in the embankment before I noticed he had carried my notebook under his arm all the way from the theatre. When I took it back our hands remained together a moment longer than the exchange required. I have made a good many measurements of hesitation since then. None has been as irritating to recall as that one. We returned separately through different streets, though no law compelled us to do so. One must allow for the appetite of neighbors.

## 1862

### E01-008 | The First Blue Ledger | 1862-01

I bought the blue ledger from a merchant who seemed distressed that I intended to use an account book for science. Expenditure occupied the left column, failure the right. Kappel inquired where I meant to put success. A frivolous question, I thought, until a specimen behaved precisely as predicted and left me with nowhere to record the result. I ruled a third column in red. There was no ceremony in it. A record that cannot accommodate its own contradiction belongs in the wastebasket, preferably before a rival finds it. The cracked neck of Lenz's first vessel remained entered against the cost of replacement. I have spent more money since, and received less instruction for it.

### E09-007 | The Price of a Seat | 1862-02

Kappel obtained a seat for me at a faculty dinner where young scholars were expected to eat their vegetables and agree at suitable intervals. Heller's colleague described the physiology of sleep without once admitting that his examples concerned only dogs. I contradicted him between the fish and the roast, with the unfortunate result that the table became interested. Afterward Kappel showed me the seating list. He had placed me beside the colleague precisely because the man was considering an assistant for his laboratory. The offer was withdrawn. I assured Kappel I preferred independence to employment and then spent the following two months borrowing instruments from men who possessed both. I did not apologize at the next dinner. I did begin asking who had been invited, which is a less noble adjustment and therefore one I seldom recount.

### E01-009 | The Orchard After Frost | 1862-03

Clara dragged me into an orchard on a raw March morning and put a knife into my hand. Beneath the bark of a tree which any romantic poet would have pronounced dead lay a narrow, obstinate green. Two cut branches came home wrapped in damp cloth. One budded. The other did nothing whatever. I asked permission to open both and examine the difference; Clara declined on the curious ground that she wished to see the first one live. For a fortnight I contrived reasons to visit her shop at the hour when the window caught the sun. I did not thank the branch for its lesson in patience. I wanted to know why its neighbor had failed, and I rather resented the surviving bud for keeping its secret.

### E09-008 | The Ledger with Two Columns | 1862-04

The blue ledger was becoming an agreeable monument to my competence until Lenz asked where I recorded the failures. I pointed to the blank spaces between successful trials. He replied that a blank space looked very like a day on which a gentleman had simply stayed in bed. Kappel gave him a ruling pen and watched with the expression of a man observing a dangerous improvement in administrative practice. We divided the page into what was intended and what had actually occurred. On the first sheet, the right-hand column was disfigured by failures. On the second, I began distinguishing a wrong hypothesis from a spoilt vessel. Soon the ledger became less flattering and more useful. I kept the first page, ink blots and all. I cannot say I welcomed correction. But I found myself less anxious about the next experiment when I knew precisely how the previous one had offended me.

### E01-010 | The Essay No One Read | 1862-05

My essay defined the living organism by the continuance of its internal arrangement rather than by its resemblance to a familiar beast. Thirty-seven pages, which I considered a model of brevity. Heller returned it with the question: would a clock qualify? I had thought the distinction too obvious to require stating. He had therefore found the exact place to apply his knife. I spent two weeks in a clockmaker's shop, where a handsome mechanism expired whenever its spring ran down and never once repaired itself. My second paper concerned maintenance, repair, and the difference between borrowed motion and organized persistence. Heller wrote 'Closer' beneath the final paragraph. I kept that sheet. I cannot say what became of his more generous compliments to other men.

### E16-003 | The Door Left Unlatched | 1862-06

Theodor's room contained a narrow bed, three portfolios and a perpetually inadequate stove. He had borrowed a lens from me to enlarge a stage design, and I went to retrieve it after supper. He had left the door unlatched because, he said, I knocked as though serving a writ. There was a bottle of ordinary red wine on the sill. We argued about whether the painted moon on a backdrop should be allowed to cast an impossible shadow. I finally kissed him to interrupt his objection. The objection resumed afterward, rather to my delight. He did not ask me to give this occurrence a name. When I left, I forgot the lens and took his scarf instead. Leonie noticed it around my neck the next morning, advised me that its color was unforgiving, and continued sewing without requiring an explanation. I resented her discretion almost as much as I benefited from it. Theodor kept the lens for another month. It produced an excellent drawing. I have never decided which of us was most pleased with the arrangement.

### E01-011 | A Dinner Without a Demonstration | 1862-07

Lenz brought a pie to Clara's Sunday table in a box intended for optical parts. I brought a drawing. She removed the drawing from beside the gravy and told us we were welcome to dinner only if we refrained from turning the potatoes into scientific examples. We managed eleven minutes. Lenz spoke about his sister's apprenticeship as a seamstress; Clara complained of customers who expected medicine to compensate for dirty water. I discovered that I did not know whether Lenz had brothers, although I had trusted him for a year with my most fragile vessels. On the walk back he asked if my mind ever stopped working. I said it stopped whenever someone served overcooked cabbage. He laughed, and thereafter never forgot to ask whether I had eaten.

### E09-009 | Wine at Clara's Table | 1862-08

Clara set a bottle of ordinary red wine beside the bread and announced that nobody would discuss an experiment until the soup was finished. I agreed, not wishing to seem a barbarian in a lady's house. Reuter spent the meal describing an elderly patient who stole pears from his own orchard because he no longer recognized its new owner. Lenz told a story about his brother's wedding. I knew nothing of weddings, and the pears seemed to invite a theory of possession, which Clara prohibited with a raised spoon. When the others left she said she enjoyed a room in which I could occasionally be a guest rather than a professor. I replied that guests were dreadful things, always requiring more wine. Nevertheless I began reserving one evening a month for her table. When an experiment threatened it, I grew unusually industrious during the preceding afternoon.

### E13-003 | The Invitation Written in Pencil | 1862-09

Leonie invited me to a small supper by writing the hour in pencil upon the reverse of a cloth merchant's bill. I arrived in the black coat my father considered nearly respectable. There were seven guests and only six proper chairs. A young violinist surrendered his seat to me with a bow so elaborate that I suspected an insult. Leonie introduced me as the man who had explained an empty jar to a full room. Clara, who knew the incident, laughed more loudly than was necessary. At dessert the violinist asked whether I meant to marry. I answered that marriage was an ingenious institution for ensuring that the least interesting questions were asked every day by the same person. Leonie said he had only asked because his sister admired my lectures. I had offended an absent woman without knowing she existed. I did not withdraw the observation. On the way home I returned to retrieve the merchant's bill, which I had left beside my plate. The pencil marks were faint and would otherwise have been thrown away.

### E01-012 | The Written Refusal | 1862-10

My father offered to place me in a clerkship where accurate figures and a respectable coat would, he said, carry me farther than disputing the nature of life. He had already written the recommendation. It stood on the mantel, folded in three, while he asked me to be practical. I thanked him for the care with which he had prepared it and returned it unread. This was not an act of courage. I was certain I could find my way without his help and rather liked proving it. In December I discovered that the examination fee exceeded my savings and sold two books I had intended to keep. My father never mentioned the letter again. Neither did I. The empty spaces on my shelf spoke with greater economy.

### E09-010 | The Lining of the Black Coat | 1862-11

My father sent me the name of a tailor who would alter a serviceable black coat. He did not offer to pay for it. The cloth was excellent, though the lining scratched my neck and one sleeve hung lower than its fellow. I accused the tailor of making philosophical judgments about my posture. Lenz inspected the seams and showed me that the coat had belonged to a taller man who bent forward when working. The tailor altered it twice without charging for the second visit. At a university reception I heard someone call the coat theatrical. I discovered that the word could be employed as praise by those who intended it as insult. My father saw me in it at Christmas and asked whether I had been appointed to a funeral. I said I wished to look as grave as the conclusions I was expected to defend. He laughed, unexpectedly. I wore it until the cuffs shone.

### E16-004 | A Table Set for Three | 1862-12

Clara invited us to supper and laid three places without commentary upon the hour at which Theodor and I arrived together. There was cabbage, a small roast and bread too coarse for a respectable faculty dinner. Theodor supplied a story about a painted waterfall that collapsed during a performance. I improved the story by explaining why the failure had been predictable. Clara told me that the failure was not the point. They continued their conversation around me while I ate in injured silence. Afterward she gave Theodor a wrapped parcel of biscuits and asked whether he had enough coal. She asked me whether I had finally repaired the cabinet catch. It took me several days to decide that she had treated us differently, and somewhat longer to realize why. Theodor said that she understood affection well enough not to make an exhibition of it. I said that she was a woman of sound practical instincts. He replied that this was the longest possible way of admitting agreement.

## 1863

### E01-013 | An Interval Miscounted | 1863-01

Lenz delivered a metronome whose wooden case had split along the back. Emil suggested it could stand in for the expensive timing apparatus we were forbidden to borrow. We measured repeated recoveries from a mild stimulus in preserved preparation and obtained intervals so tidy that I suspected fraud. Emil demonstrated the deception without intending to: the pendulum slowed by a fraction when the table shook. The table shook whenever the furnace below was fed. We moved the apparatus to the window and repeated the work. The new results were untidy, which is to say they were useful. I bought the damaged metronome rather than return it; the crack made the instrument unfit for vanity and excellent for memory.

### E09-011 | A Man Who Could Count | 1863-02

Reuter ruined a week's work by confusing two bottles whose contents had settled to the same pale color. I expected a confession, perhaps a satisfying evening of reproach. Instead he made a list of everything we could still infer and put it beside the stove. The best conclusion was modest: we had no result. Heller read the list and praised Reuter's accuracy. I protested that accuracy without discovery was clerical virtue. Heller asked how much of the previous week's work I had privately hoped to count as a success before the bottles were checked. I answered with considerable dignity and a number I later reduced in the blue ledger. Reuter had the decency not to observe the revision. From that winter onward, I could be intolerable about method even when the method prevented me from being impressive. It was an expensive preference, but it was mine.

### E01-014 | A Funeral Register | 1863-03

The clerk at the death register would not let me copy a man's last recorded breath. I possessed no claim of kinship, and I pointed out that kinship seldom improves arithmetic. The clerk remained impenetrable. Kappel returned with me bearing a properly signed letter and a look of disappointment which I found more tiresome than the first refusal. The interval between the final breath and the entry of death varied with weather, witnesses, and the reliability of a pen. I had hoped for the precise frontier of life. Instead, I found officials moving a boundary to suit the hour at which someone summoned them. Kappel said the mourners were not experimental material. I told him the register, at least, had volunteered for examination.

### E13-004 | The Ribbon and the Name | 1863-04

A length of blue ribbon lay across Leonie's worktable beside a painted crown for a provincial theatre. I told her the crown was historically absurd. She replied that the king would be seen from forty paces and a historically accurate crown would look like a biscuit. She then showed me a ledger in which payments for costumes were entered under the actors' names, never the seamstresses'. I had come to ask for black velvet to cover an instrument. Instead I spent an hour examining receipts signed by men who had never held a needle. I proposed she publish a correction. She said the theatre manager would dismiss her and employ three poorer women the following week. I advised her to keep copies of everything. She said, with some accuracy, that this was the advice of a man not obliged to feed a household from the wages in question. Lenz later found me measuring the velvet against the instrument as though a satisfactory fit might settle the argument. I entered Leonie's name in my notebook beneath the dimensions.

### E09-012 | The Unsent Recommendation | 1863-05

Kappel offered to write a recommendation for a stipend that would pay the rent and spare me a second winter without proper heating. The form required a statement of character. He read aloud the phrase 'obedient to instruction' and waited for me to object. I obliged. For an hour we altered the sentence until it said I completed assigned work but disputed its assumptions. My father, asked to countersign my application, returned it with a note that the second clause would cost me the stipend. It did. Kappel retained the draft in his cabinet and found me other temporary access to the instruments. I declared the lost money insignificant. That evening I counted what remained in my purse twice. Pride has always been more agreeable when someone else supplies the coal; that fact has never persuaded me to surrender it.

### E01-015 | Clara's Broken Jar | 1863-06

Six jars fell from a shelf in Clara's dispensary. She swore at the broken labels before she mourned the spilled medicines, for a known loss may be replaced and an unknown mixture may poison someone. Lenz repaired the bracket. I imposed upon the surviving bottles a numbering scheme which Clara condemned as a professor's method of making every simple act require spectacles. We argued, reduced it to two marks on glass and a single page beside the till, then tried it for a month. It saved a misidentified batch during a crowded morning. Clara told me so while counting coins, without looking up. I went away in excellent humor, which I attributed to vindication and not to the rare pleasure of having been of use outside my own laboratory.

### E09-013 | A Dish Returned Clean | 1863-08

A dish Clara had lent me returned smelling of preservative. She detected it before removing the wrapping and deposited the article in my hands as if it carried a charge of treason. I offered to replace it. She demanded that I clean it properly and bring it back myself. When I returned, she was treating a child whose mother could not pay. I waited with the dish for nearly an hour while Clara worked. Only afterward did she inspect the glass and accept it. She told me a gentleman's offer of money could not restore the hour another person must spend correcting his negligence. I considered a formidable reply and discovered I had none worth making. I began keeping two sets of glassware, one for the laboratory and one for Clara's table. She never thanked me for this elementary separation, and her failure to do so was entirely justified, a judgment I did not express to her.

### E16-005 | The Photograph Not Taken | 1863-08

On an excursion to Augsburg, Theodor proposed a photograph. The operator had arranged a velvet chair and two potted palms as though respectable companionship could be manufactured out of upholstery. I would not sit beside Theodor for the proposed double portrait. The operator had already asked which of us was the elder brother, and I disliked the readiness with which a stranger sought a category for us. Theodor offered to stand behind the chair, his hand on its carved back. I declined that too. We were photographed separately. In his portrait his hair refuses to lie flat over the left ear; in mine I look as though I had been ordered to attend my own memorial service. Theodor paid for both and said very little on the train. I told myself I had resisted vulgar sentimentality. In my pocket was a receipt for two photographs, and on the reverse he had written the time our train arrived. I still possess that receipt. The portraits were never placed together in a frame.

### E01-016 | A Visitor at the Back Door | 1863-09

My father came without his carriage and stood at the back door while I heated broth over a spirit lamp. He had heard I was selling books. I said the rumor was exaggerated, which it was only in the plural. He asked whether I wished to eat with the family that Sunday. I replied that I had been invited elsewhere, and saw at once that I had made another household sound like a challenge. Before leaving he examined the cracked metronome on my desk and asked if it still kept time. 'Only when properly placed,' I answered. He put down a parcel of good paper and said that was true of men as well. I used the paper for my thesis. I never told him.

### E01-017 | The Thesis in the Snow | 1863-12

I carried my thesis across the courtyard under my coat during the first wet snow of December. Reuter had checked the tables; Heller had removed three flattering adjectives with his own pencil. At the office I discovered that one page of diagrams was missing. It had remained on my bed, beside an untouched supper. Emil ran back for it while I waited on a corridor bench beneath a badly painted saint. When he returned he was breathless and furious that I had entrusted so much to a loose sheet. I submitted the packet a few minutes before the clerk locked his drawer. I remember chiefly that my feet were cold and that Emil, having rescued the work, refused to accompany me to the tavern.

## 1864

### E09-014 | The Question Withheld | 1864-02

Before the defense Heller arranged a rehearsal at which the faculty's most unpleasant questions were to be asked in advance. He turned my thesis over and asked whether the most elegant hypothesis might be false for an exceedingly dull reason. I had a reply prepared, elaborate enough to silence a bishop. Then I saw his pencil resting on a table of intervals whose apparatus had shifted in cold weather. I withheld the reply and asked for the measurements. Reuter looked relieved; Heller looked almost disappointed. We repeated the table and lost a pleasing symmetry. At the defense I presented the uglier figures without explaining how recently I had ceased preferring the handsome ones. My silence had been tactical at first. The corrected table was better evidence. Both facts remain true, however much biographers prefer their heroes to grow virtuous at a single sitting.

### E01-018 | The Defense of a Boundary | 1864-03

At my defense, one examiner asked about dormant seeds; another about sleeping men. I preferred the first question, because a seed makes fewer demands upon a philosopher's conscience. Heller wanted an observation that would defeat my thesis, an extraordinarily ungenerous thing to demand of a candidate already obliged to defend it. I supplied one. Afterward Reuter shook my hand, looking so grave that I was briefly pleased to have inspired envy. His mother was in hospital. We walked there with my thesis under my arm, and I carried it all the way back because no one had the sense to offer me a shelf. The doctorate followed. I was scarcely beyond my student years and had earned the title I had been using in argument for some years. My pleasure was considerable, even if it was not wholly decorous.

### E01-019 | The Professorial Coat | 1864-05

The faculty conferred a professorship upon me with such reckless haste that I have since been accused of falsifying my age. Heller examined my cuffs and declared that a professor should look wealthy enough to endure disagreement. Kappel found me an office with a window facing a brick wall and a door which refused to close in wet weather. I purchased a black coat, second-hand but admirably severe, and placed the blue ledger in the upper drawer. No student came to my first office hour. I prepared objections to six imaginary theses, each worse than the last, and remained until the caretaker extinguished the corridor lamps. Promotion had made me important in the eyes of the institution. It had not yet persuaded the institution to send me an audience.

### E09-015 | The Empty Audience Book | 1864-07

After my appointment I placed a register beside the office door for students who wished to attend a weekly colloquium. On the first Thursday two names appeared, one a false signature evidently supplied by Kappel, the other a student who wanted permission to leave early. I prepared the entire lecture nonetheless. On the third Thursday there were seven listeners. I had abandoned the register and begun speaking in the corridor before the session, disputing Heller's latest proposition loudly enough that students followed to hear whether one of us might draw blood. Heller later observed that controversy had become my advertising department. I replied that the university's own notices were less informative. Once the attendance improved, I stopped quarrelling at the doorway quite so often. I retained the habit of starting with a provocation. It was reliable, unlike institutional announcements, and much less costly than printing handbills.

### E01-020 | The Last Free Afternoon | 1864-08

Lenz announced that he would be married in autumn and might no longer be available for repairs at a moment's notice. I congratulated him by asking whether his intended bride understood the demands of sealed glass joints. Clara said that if I wished to preserve the friendship I should say something less absurd. We walked beside the river, ate black bread and watched two children set paper boats on the current. Lenz described a little workshop he hoped to rent. For the first time I understood that he intended a life not built around my apparatus. I offered him payment in advance for the next year's work, an offer he declined because he did not wish to owe me a year he had not yet lived. I admired the refusal and disliked it.

### E16-006 | A Place beside the Window | 1864-09

Following my appointment, I bought a respectable writing table and transferred the specimens from the window shelf to a locked cupboard. Theodor came to see the changes and stood looking at the clear space where his portfolio had formerly rested. He congratulated me on the appointment without enthusiasm. I told him that faculty rooms required arrangement and that the drawings would be safer elsewhere. He said he had not asked whether they were safe. I answered with an account of the risks attached to loose papers and spilled reagents. We ate cold meat while the new table occupied half the room between us. Before leaving he set a wooden ruler beside the window, precisely where it had always lain. A week later I moved it to a drawer to make room for an anatomical plate. The ruler remained there through the entire winter, although I used inferior instruments rather than fetch it. Had I been asked at the time, I should have insisted the matter concerned only furniture.

### E02-001 | An Earnest Student | 1864-10

Henry Frankenstein remained after my lecture while the others went to find their coats. He had an awkward habit of placing a question in front of him as though it were a fragile article and asking permission to touch it. His concern was not whether life could be measured, but whether the measurement would alter the thing measured. I gave him a badly copied diagram and told him to return it with three objections. The following week he brought seven, four good and three merely ambitious. He had marked the margins with a neatness that annoyed me until I discovered he had corrected one of my units. I began leaving the door unlatched on Thursdays. Henry interpreted this as encouragement; for once his interpretation was correct.

### E10-001 | The Corrected Diagram | 1864-11

Henry returned the anatomical drawing I had lent him with a correction in red ink and a paragraph in which he carefully assured me that his alteration did not detract from the general argument. It did, of course, and I told him so. He looked miserable until I asked whether he had tested the new relation on another specimen. He had brought measurements in his pocket. I kept him until the caretaker called closing time and sent him away with three more drawings, each containing an error I had deliberately left in it. The following week he identified two and defended the third because the evidence supported my original version. I admired that defense. I also disliked the speed with which he was learning to dispute me. I had wanted a pupil who would exceed the syllabus. I had not reflected upon the inconvenience of his doing so in my presence.

### E02-002 | The Anatomist's Conditions | 1864-12

Henry wished to work after hours in the anatomy hall. He believed I could simply grant permission. I explained that a professor of philosophy does not possess the keys to every corpse in Bavaria. Heller, who did possess influence, demanded a written purpose and a named supervisor. Henry prepared a letter so earnest that it sounded like a confession. I struck out the word 'conquest' and replaced it with 'observation.' Heller approved a restricted afternoon. Henry resented the limit; I resented asking for it. We used the afternoon to examine the conditions under which tissue ceased responding to stimulation. The most useful result was that neither of us could honestly say where cessation became final.

### E13-005 | A Private Table for Henry | 1864-12

Henry had not yet become accustomed to my lectures when Leonie permitted us the use of a side room behind the hall. It contained a cracked oval mirror and a table too narrow for the anatomical plates he wished to unroll. He had prepared a painstaking objection to one of my definitions of life, and he read it as though an examiner were waiting to fail him. I took the paper, folded it where his argument became redundant, and asked him to begin at the fold. He objected that the earlier paragraphs justified the conclusion. Leonie entered carrying a lantern and said that a man who must carry all his evidence into every conversation should travel with a cart. Henry laughed, then managed to state the distinction in twelve words. I remembered the twelve words, not the obedient pages. When he left, Leonie asked whether I had invited the student to learn or to admire. I replied that he might do both if he proved industrious. She told me to return the mirror before Friday. It was, I think, her way of conceding the point.

## 1865

### E10-002 | The Wrong Kind of Apology | 1865-01

I corrected Henry sharply before six students after he confused the cessation of movement with the cessation of organization. He returned the next week having rewritten his argument, yet he would not speak. I asked whether the problem was his new evidence. He said he did not intend to be made an exhibit twice. Heller, who had overheard this, advised me to learn the difference between testing a thesis and testing a man's willingness to be humiliated. I disliked his formulation and told Henry he could conduct the next discussion himself. He did so with infuriating seriousness and surprised the room with an objection to my own lecture. I let it stand. Later I told him he had behaved quite competently. I did not apologize. He returned to office hours, and I made a practice of reserving certain corrections for the walk afterward. It was not gentleness. I wanted him thinking in my presence rather than guarding against it.

### E02-003 | A Question Under the Lamp | 1865-02

Henry arrived with the skull of a bird, wrapped in a handkerchief, and asked whether a living creature could be constructed from correct parts alone. I told him that a clock assembled from perfect wheels does nothing without their proper relations, and he objected that the metaphor proved too much. Our argument continued until the lamp smoked. Henry sketched a scheme for joining separate tissues; I drew instead a process by which organization might arise within a small volume. We did not resolve the distinction. The following morning my notes contained the phrase 'formation rather than assembly,' but I could not tell whether it had been mine or his. I left the authorship blank. It would not remain a harmless uncertainty.

### E13-006 | The Two Glasses of Gin | 1865-03

Leonie offered me gin after a meeting in which a committee member had suggested that my investigations would be more acceptable if I called them demonstrations of divine craftsmanship. She filled two small glasses, then took one away when I said I had never been drunk in my life. I protested that this was no reason to be parsimonious. Clara appeared to collect a packet of dressings and observed that men who advertised themselves as immune to a weakness were usually planning to indulge it. I lifted the remaining glass and declared that gin was my one concession to human frailty. They were not impressed. The liquor was coarse, and the lemon peel had been cut with a pair of costume shears. For all that, I remember the kitchen heat, the smell of starch, and the extraordinary relief of having no one ask me to defend a theory for nearly an hour. Thereafter I sometimes described gin as a weakness. It was considerably easier than naming the company.

### E02-004 | An Unpaid Lecture | 1865-04

Clara asked me to speak to apprentices about the difference between a medicine's label and its effects. Henry accompanied me, expecting an intellectual debate. What he received was a queue of tired women and young men with ink on their cuffs, none of whom wished to hear about Prometheus. I explained the error in a dosage ledger, using Clara's paired bottle marks, and the room paid attention. Afterward Henry said he had learned more from their questions than from my philosophy seminar. I told him he had an unfortunate enthusiasm for praising the obvious. Clara paid us in supper. Henry stayed to wash the cups, a courtesy I regarded as excessive until I noticed how tired she was.

### E02-005 | The Correspondence Packet | 1865-06

Reuter sent accounts of patients recovered from conditions which their physicians had called hopeless. Henry wanted to make them evidence for his theory of reanimation. I objected that none had been proved dead, and Henry objected that I was protecting a definition as if it were a fortress. We wrote separate replies. Mine contained a list of necessary observations. His contained almost the same list and a question about Reuter's mother. Reuter answered Henry and omitted me entirely. I told the young man that social graces often purchase the appearance of intellectual credit. He had the impertinence to point out that I had learned the contents of the answer by reading over his shoulder. This was true and irrelevant. I borrowed the packet for another week.

### E10-003 | The Poorhouse Petition | 1865-07

Reuter brought a request for a practical lecture at the poorhouse. The superintendent wished us to demonstrate new principles of circulation and had offered living residents as convenient examples. Henry was delighted by the prospect of cases not confined to anatomy books. Clara asked who had consented, and Reuter answered that the superintendent had signed the invitation. She placed it beside a patient's unpaid bill and inquired whether either scrap of paper was the patient's own hand. I began to object that we were discussing demonstration rather than injury. In the end I went to the poorhouse with a blackboard and no instruments. Henry was disappointed; the residents asked better questions than the superintendent. I did not abandon the conviction that knowledge often demands intrusion. At my next lecture I left the rear door unlatched. Two listeners went out during the introductory remarks. The others stayed and argued.

### E16-007 | A Sunday without the Laboratory | 1865-08

Theodor obtained a Sunday free from work and asked me to travel out to a country inn. He had brought a small bag containing bread, pears and the drawing he wished to show me. I arrived two hours late with a bottle of preserved tissue wrapped in my coat. He did not immediately speak. We took a table outdoors. His drawing showed the theatre wall after rain, its plaster cracking in slow curves. I described the bottle's unusual response to temperature before I had properly looked at his work. He folded the paper. I offered to explain that my experiment might fail altogether if left overnight. He said, quietly, that the experiments were always at the point of failure and there would never be a day sufficiently unimportant to keep free. I replied that he had chosen a profession in which performances also dictated one's hours. He said that performances ended. I remember the pears more clearly than the journey home. One had bruised in the bag and we cut away the black portion with his penknife. Neither of us called the afternoon a quarrel. We knew perfectly well that it was one.

### E02-006 | The Key Returned | 1865-09

Kappel demanded the return of the cabinet key. I accused him of joining the procession of cowards who feared what an unlocked drawer might reveal. He let me finish, an ominous courtesy, then showed me the inventory register. For years he had signed my loans under his own name; now instruments were missing and the signatures exposed him, not me, to an inquiry. I put the key on his desk. He did not offer absolution and I did not offer an apology suitable for public consumption. Instead I copied the instruments I still had permission to examine and returned them by the appointed hour. At the door he asked about delayed recovery, remembering the experiment for which he had first trusted me. I have known men who declared lifelong loyalty and remembered much less.

### E10-004 | An Account Against the Wall | 1865-10

Lenz arrived with an invoice and refused another promise of payment in the form of future scientific fame. My new laboratory required glass joints he could have sold to paying customers, and his marriage had not abolished the cost of coal. I said I had invested in his skill. He asked whether he might invest my admiration at the baker's. Clara, who had accompanied him, said nothing. I sold an engraved instrument I seldom used and paid two-thirds of the account. Lenz gave me a further month for the balance, but he no longer accepted orders without written terms. I called his policy officious. He continued supplying the finest vessels I could obtain. To this day I distrust a man who asks for blind faith in a partnership. I also resent being reminded that the distrust was taught to me by a glassblower.

### E25-001 | The Question Beneath the Twitch | 1865-10

The nerve moved when I applied the current and continued to move after the animal from which it came could no longer answer any question. A visitor called it the soul reluctant to depart. Reuter spoke of a lingering spark. Neither had looked at the wires. On my table lay a dish with a chipped rim, two damp corks, and a fork of brass blackened at the contact. The little lever scratched a more intelligible argument than the gentlemen. I asked Clara to read the thermometer at the glass, then wrote three words in my blue ledger: movement, relation, person. I made separate boxes around them. She wanted to know why I had arranged them in that order. I answered that a person was usually the last thing the physiologist knew and the first thing the theologian presumed. My wit was wasted on a woman trying to prevent the notebook becoming wet. When I later gave the boxes scholarly titles, I did not admit they began as a precaution against a puddle.

### E02-007 | The Small Green Vessel | 1865-11

Lenz made a greenish glass vessel from cullet he had intended to discard. Its base was imperfect and caught the lamplight in a narrow sickle. I asked whether the tint might harm the specimens. He asked whether I intended to study the specimen or the tint. Clara had brought dormant fragments of plant tissue for observation. Some revived; some did not. I began to suspect that organization might be induced by surroundings that did more than preserve it. I record this only as the origin of a line of thought, not as the origin of a recipe. When Lenz asked what I meant to grow, I said, 'Something that knows it has grown.' He called that an unnecessarily difficult commission.

### E13-007 | The Devil's Better Argument | 1865-12

Kappel found me marking a sermon which accused modern investigators of imitating the Devil. I had underlined the accusation twice, not because I agreed with its theology but because its author had finally managed to invent an interesting description of my work. Leonie, visiting with a parcel of repaired curtains, examined the pamphlet and proposed that the Devil had at least been allowed to ask questions before being condemned. Kappel reminded us that the author was chiefly concerned with public safety. I replied that safety was a poor substitute for argument, and he asked whether I had ever refused to test an idea because the test might ruin another person's health. I said the circumstances would matter. It was not a victory, but neither was it a retreat. Later Leonie stitched a tiny black cape for a theatre devil and left the remnant on my table. I kept it between pages of the sermon. When a similar figure appeared among my little people, I did not entirely forget the scrap of cloth.

### E19-001 | The First Beautiful Error | 1865-12

The green vessel made Lenz proud of his craft, and I disliked spoiling his satisfaction with an unsatisfactory occupant. A pale little crescent had appeared against the glass, exquisite in outline, quite without a blemish visible to the lens. Beside it, in a crooked vessel Clara had brought, another growth showed a disagreeable swelling. I displayed the crescent to a visiting naturalist and put the crooked specimen beneath the bench. Within the week the crescent had stopped altogether. The swollen specimen continued its obscure business. Clara placed both vessels on my table without a word, which was an intolerable form of criticism. I entered the results under separate headings, Beauty and Continuance. Lenz asked why I had not merely written Success and Failure. Because, I explained, I had yet to decide which result deserved which word. I turned the beautiful vessel toward the wall when he left.

## 1866

### E02-008 | The Demonstration Declined | 1866-01

Heller offered me a public lecture provided I supplied a result and not another elegant conjecture. Henry wished to show our most responsive samples. I refused. They had responded on three occasions in four different ways, and a crowded room would invite us to call the most flattering outcome the true one. I lectured instead on vitalism, to twenty-six spectators who diminished to fifteen before the lamps were extinguished. Henry looked as though we had been sentenced. I had hoped for thunderous applause and had the decency to be vexed by its absence. Heller said an honest dullness was preferable to a splendid falsehood. I replied that he had made a profession of proving it. This was unjust, and he laughed, which made it worse.

### E10-005 | The Lecture That Returned in Print | 1866-02

A pamphlet from another faculty reproduced an argument from my January lecture, including a mistaken spelling of a Latin term that had survived in the written notes. The author made no mention of me. Henry demanded that we accuse the man at once. Kappel advised a private inquiry; Heller suggested the error might have passed through students' notebooks without deliberate theft. I wrote a devastating letter and left it in my drawer. Then I prepared a corrected version of the argument and had copies deposited under my name. The unauthorized author published again two months later, this time with an awkward acknowledgment. I considered that partial justice. Henry asked why I had not sent the accusation. I replied that I had preferred an enduring record to a moment's revenge. I neglected to mention the evenings I had spent imagining the revenge in gratifying detail.

### E25-002 | The Margin Too Narrow | 1866-02

A colleague returned my preliminary argument with three pages of objection and a fold which passed directly through the diagram of a nerve. He disliked the phrase by which I had declared an animal remembered its last impression. He was correct: I had shown a changed response, not remembrance. I spent an evening inventing replies that would have embarrassed his ancestors, then substituted a less splendid sentence. When Heller saw the revised page he approved it without asking who had obliged me to improve it. I was annoyed that sound advice might pass for his own influence. At supper I quoted the correspondence so wickedly that Clara asked to see the parts I had omitted. I said she would find them dull. She insisted. I produced the letters and watched her read them in their proper order. Her judgement did not differ from his. It was a disagreeable evening for vanity and an exceptionally good one for the manuscript.

### E02-009 | The Nation on the Noticeboard | 1866-03

The notices about war were posted beside announcements of examinations and room changes, as though armies marched according to the academic calendar. Lenz worried about cousins who might be called up. Henry spoke of patriotic duty with the conviction of a man whose own hands were occupied by safer questions. I told him that nations were exceptionally large organisms and that governments shared with surgeons an alarming faith in amputation. He took offense. Later I bought extra coal because I feared transport would become erratic. Whatever opinions I possessed about states, they did not keep a laboratory warm. Lenz remembered my practical calculation long after Henry forgot my remark.

### E19-002 | The Gentleman Who Paid for a Face | 1866-04

A collector sent two coins and requested a drawing of the finished creature I had mentioned, most injudiciously, over supper. There was no finished creature. There was a diagram of a symmetrical head that had never possessed more than the possibility of one. Friedrich Behr, who could turn my atrocious ink into legible type, suggested putting the word proposal beneath the sketch. The collector removed it in his imagination and congratulated me upon an achievement. I accepted the payment for the drawing and returned his request to see the living specimen. Afterward I spent three nights trying to make the next trial resemble the charcoal outline. Behr observed that I had allowed a gentleman's purse to become an anatomical authority. I told her the purse had purchased precisely the opposite privilege, namely the right to be disappointed later. I did not return the coins. We needed glass more urgently than we needed spotless consciences.

### E10-006 | The Cardboard Curtain | 1866-05

Henry observed an early growth preparation and immediately began offering improvements to the apparatus. I was pleased for the first ten minutes and exasperated thereafter. He leaned over a vessel during a phase when temperature mattered, and I snatched his sleeve away before he touched the glass. He called my alarm unreasonable. I placed a cardboard screen between the observation table and the doorway and insisted that everyone entering announce himself. Henry said I had turned science into theatre. 'Yes,' I told him, 'and now I shall decide who steps upon the stage.' The screen remained after he departed. It kept drafts from the vessels, which was useful, and curious eyes from my failures, which was more useful than I cared to say. The screen remained when we no longer needed its protection from drafts. I had Lenz paint its outer face black.

### E02-010 | Henry's Unsigned Page | 1866-06

Henry left a set of drawings beneath my door showing connections between separate organs. The pages contained no signature, though his precise numbering made authorship unmistakable. I corrected a faulty relation and returned them without comment. He confronted me three days later, convinced that silence meant contempt. I told him I had judged the drawing, not its author. He replied that authorship was precisely what frightened him: he feared producing something that would live and therefore belong to neither of us. I laughed at the word 'belong.' He did not. It was one of the first times I saw him hear a moral claim in a problem I considered mechanical. I kept a copy of the diagram and did not tell him so.

### E13-008 | An Audience of One | 1866-07

I prepared a magnificent rebuke to a rival's theory and arranged for Henry to hear it before the lecture. He listened through the first three paragraphs, then asked whether I intended to prove the man wrong or make his supporters uncomfortable. I said that one need not choose. He pointed out a weakness in the fourth paragraph, where my rhetoric exceeded the observed result. I removed the passage and delivered the remainder to a hall so poorly attended that the doorkeeper could count the listeners without moving his lips. Leonie sat at the back repairing a torn glove. Afterward she said the lecture had improved at precisely the point where I stopped pretending an opponent's ignorance was experimental evidence. Henry looked insufferably pleased. I wrote the correction into the blue ledger, then spent most of the evening composing a remark clever enough to convince them that I had conceded nothing. The remark was excellent. Unfortunately they had already gone home.

### E19-003 | A Vessel without a Witness | 1866-07

Henry found me watching a minute fleck rise each time I tapped the glass. I called it anticipation. He asked whether it rose when I did not tap. A particularly tiresome question, since no one had proposed holding a séance for a bit of tissue. He folded a card around the side of the vessel, moved the tapping point and found that the fleck no longer rose. He had removed a current I had overlooked. I informed him that invalidating my interpretation did not invalidate the observation, and he agreed, smiling in a way that suggested he had expected a battle. We spent the rest of the afternoon making marks on cork and moving a watch from one end of the bench to the other. When he departed I wrote his suggestion in my ledger. I did not write that he had been pleased to have caught me. That would have made the record unnecessarily human.

### E25-003 | A Man Carrying His Hat | 1866-07

Reuter told me about a patient who had recovered speech after a long silence. He wished to call the words proof that the soul had returned to its proper place. I asked what the patient had done with his hands while unable to speak. No one had recorded it. In the hospital corridor there was the smell of boiled linen and the clatter of a pail dragged over stone. The man appeared before we had finished discussing him, carrying his hat and objecting that physicians spoke about him as though he had already been buried. Henry asked whether he wanted to speak further. I, being more anxious for the facts, asked three questions in succession. The patient answered the first and departed with his dignity. We learned less than I wanted. Henry said he had learned enough. I later wrote that the absence of speech proves less than the examiner supposes. I did not describe how readily I had neglected the speaker himself.

### E10-007 | The Unsigned Calculation | 1866-08

Henry left a calculation among my papers showing an alternative relationship between the mass of a living preparation and the time required for its recovery. I found an error in the last line, corrected it, and used the amended figure in a private discussion with Heller. Henry overheard enough to believe I had adopted the result without naming him. He was correct. I told him that a student's idea becomes part of his teacher's work when the teacher makes it serviceable. He asked whether the same rule would apply when the student proved the teacher wrong. I had no satisfactory answer ready. That evening I added his initials to the page and left it where he would find it. He never thanked me, and I never repeated the performance. My admiration for his courage did not lessen my appetite for ownership. Both continued to grow.

### E02-011 | The Empty Dish | 1866-09

For six weeks I inspected a sealed dish that appeared to contain nothing of interest. Clara advised me to discard it, then changed her mind because I was so eager to agree. Under an oblique light we saw minute structures along one edge, too disordered to call organs and too persistent to call sediment. I fetched Lenz, who pointed out that the dish had been tilted since the previous month. We levelled a second specimen and continued observing both. Neither produced the miracle I anticipated. The first did, however, make its growth follow a path different from that of the second. My ledger acquired an untidy sketch of two branching lines. Later I remembered the sketch more accurately than the specimen.

### E16-008 | The Drawing Returned | 1866-10

Theodor received an offer to prepare architectural drawings in another city. I met him at Leonie's workroom, where half-completed costumes hung like people unwilling to commit themselves to a profession. He showed me the terms of employment. They were good, although not so good that I could not find objections. He asked whether I wished him to decline. The room contained an empty chair and a bowl of pins. I considered telling him that the laboratory would soon produce results worth witnessing. Instead I explained that advancement ought not to be sacrificed to an arrangement whose terms neither of us had properly established. He repeated the word arrangement as though testing a badly made hinge. He returned my lens wrapped in the theatre drawing on which we had first disagreed. I congratulated him on his future and left before Leonie could introduce sympathy into the discussion. Three months later I discovered that I had been consulting his notes on perspective while marking an anatomical proof. His absence had become useful to me as an excuse for a number of errors which were wholly mine.

### E19-004 | The Little Arm That Wasn't | 1866-10

For eleven days I drew a splendid little arm, bent at the elbow, terminating in what might almost have been a hand. I drew it from the same place at the same hour, with the lamp to my left. Lenz arrived carrying a repaired stopper and, without consulting my scientific dignity, lifted the vessel onto a felt pad. The arm vanished. He rotated it once more and showed me an innocent membrane, folded by a distortion in his own curved glass. I called the workmanship criminal. He asked whether I should prefer the glassblower to have manufactured the limb itself. The replacement vessel had one flat wall, an atrocity to look upon and a great improvement to the eye. I kept the old sketches. When I saw the handsome false arm again, I remembered not how I had been deceived but how very quickly I had wished to be.

### E10-008 | A Door Measured Twice | 1866-11

Kappel warned me that a committee member had begun asking about deliveries to the private rooms. I accused him of enjoying the role of sentry. Clara had heard a second rumor from a supplier, one concerning not the apparatus but the safety of our sealed vessels. I measured the room doors, arranged for a second lock, and composed a statement about harmless botanical investigation. It was accurate as far as it went and designed to make further questions appear unnecessary. When Kappel saw the statement he said I had managed to write the truth so that nobody could find it. I admired the description enough to repeat it later as a joke. The lock deterred visitors and delayed Lenz one cold morning while a vessel overheated. We installed an emergency latch. Secrecy had protected my work and endangered it within the same fortnight. I kept the lock.

### E02-012 | The Winter Inventory | 1866-12

At year's end we took stock. Lenz's figures showed how much I owed him, Clara's which samples had lost their labels, mine the discouraging number of failures that I preferred to call incomplete trials. We agreed no undated vessel could constitute evidence. It was a good rule, and I disliked its immediate application to three preparations in which I had invested considerable hope. The next morning Lenz's wife left bread, cheese, and a note instructing me to eat before extinguishing the second lamp. I ate the bread standing at my bench. The cheese was excellent, the note intolerably familiar. I kept the note under a weight for weeks, ostensibly because it was the only clean scrap of paper within reach.

### E25-004 | Two Temperatures | 1866-12

My first beautiful chart was wrong. Across the paper the responses declined with an orderliness sufficient to please a faculty, provided nobody inquired what temperature existed at the glass rather than in my intentions. Clara inquired. She touched the vessel, then pointed to the second thermometer. Lenz brought a small support that would permit a reading without the observer warming the vessel with his hand. His thumb left soot beside the screw. I drew a less agreeable curve and labelled the first one unfit for citation. Yet I kept it, for the discredited line was remarkably handsome. When I drafted the paper's methods, I wrote proudly of independent readings and rejected preparations. I never described how long I had stared at the wrong curve before excluding it. I had sacrificed elegance to accuracy, and even then found myself judging the sacrifice by how elegantly I could explain it.

## 1867

### E10-009 | The Vessel That Would Not Answer | 1867-01

A preparation gave no coherent response for nineteen successive observations. I had begun speaking of it to Lenz as though the vessel were deliberately withholding consent. Clara asked whether the heating schedule had changed. I replied that the schedule had not changed and produced my ledger as proof. She pointed out that I had recorded intended temperatures rather than those taken at the glass. We measured again and found a difference sufficient to ruin my interpretation. I scraped my proposed account from the slate and ordered another week of observations. The preparation's behavior altered after the correction, though I could not prove that the correction alone caused it. I remember Clara's forefinger on the wrong column of the ledger. I purchased a second thermometer for Clara to read and was disagreeably attentive whenever its figures differed from mine.

### E19-005 | The Dullest Sunday | 1867-01

I had been in the laboratory since Saturday, troubling a stubborn vessel with inspections that produced nothing but a sore neck. At nine the church bells began. I was tempted to declare the entire culture dead and salvage my Sunday by declaring an enemy defeated. Clara called to fetch me for an inventory of ordinary medicines. I followed her because the lamp oil had run out and because she offered coffee without discussing my theories. We counted bottles, argued about a missing label, and ate hard bread with cheese. When I returned at dusk the vessel was precisely as it had been. I was absurdly gratified by this evidence that the world could continue to resist me without requiring my constant supervision. On Monday it moved. I would have preferred it to do so while I was watching, naturally.

### E02-013 | The First Coherent Response | 1867-02

I shall not describe the preparation of the seed. The formula is not relevant to the recollection and I have no intention of donating it to posterity. What matters is that one minute developing form turned toward an intermittent shadow and continued to orient itself after the shadow was removed. Lenz suggested a mechanical response to heat. Clara changed the light and asked us to try again. We did. The little form did not behave like the first series of samples. I remained at the bench until my knees trembled, then went out to buy more candles. The grocer asked whether I had been drinking. I had not, which made the question particularly irritating. I wrote 'possible organized response' in the blue ledger and underlined 'possible.'

### E25-005 | The Drawer I Kept Locked | 1867-02

I finished a section on ordinary spinal actions while, behind the cardboard screen, a developing little form had just followed a shadow beyond its first appearance. I underlined 'possible' in my private ledger. Its ink showed through the thin leaf. The public paper contained frogs, nerves, the persistence of irritability, and not one word about that figure. Henry asked whether I truly believed our usual experiments had failed to disclose a soul. Certainly, I said; a scientist ought not falsify the present merely because he entertains a more interesting future. I closed the drawer when he looked toward it. Afterwards I compared the two notebooks and felt a peculiar satisfaction. One could tell nothing untrue and still arrange a story so that the reader never suspected how much had been omitted. At the time I thought this a distinction between legitimate subjects. Years later I sometimes wondered whether those who called me secretive had understood it perfectly.

### E10-010 | A Queen Without a Court | 1867-03

After the first coherent response and before I had cultivated the others, the Queen occupied a small platform alone. I placed two objects where she could choose between them, intending to test a preference for height. She moved neither. She stood at the far edge with her back to me while I made increasingly persuasive adjustments. Lenz suggested that my arrangement resembled a trap. I laughed, then moved my hands from the vessel and waited. She crossed to the lower object when she was no longer under my shadow. I wrote 'avoidance of overhead obstruction.' Clara wrote 'possibly of you' in the margin. The distinction was not resolved. I have always enjoyed exercising command. My first sovereign subject made the enjoyment more interesting by failing to obey, and I grew absurdly eager to understand the terms of her refusal.

### E19-006 | A Face Too Complete | 1867-03

The little figure now possessed the beginnings of a face. One side was finer than the other, and I wasted a morning attempting to view the discrepancy as a trick of reflected light. Henry considered its response to an approaching shadow more important than the uneven jaw. Clara asked whether I intended to accuse it of bad breeding. I found both remarks provincial. Nevertheless I abandoned the drawing with its invented symmetry and sat beside the vessel until the shadows lengthened. When I approached with a silver mirror the creature turned away from the brightest patch. If it could choose its comfort over my preferred view, it had advanced beyond any portrait I might devise. I did not describe the episode in these sentimental terms to Henry. I merely forbade him to touch the lamp.

### E02-014 | Her Majesty's Refusal | 1867-04

The Queen was the first. I named her days after she had begun to live, when it became clear she had no intention of behaving like a laboratory ornament. She favored one ledge and returned to it after every change of arrangement. I rotated the vessel; Clara argued that light would explain the preference. The little sovereign ascended the ledge once more, surveyed my enormous face, and withdrew to an entirely different corner. I said she had made her choice, a scientific description to which Clara objected. Perhaps the creature only wanted shade. It is difficult to be certain when one's kingdom is made of glass and one's creator is forever breathing upon it. I spent that night in a chair beside her and woke with an excellent idea and a thoroughly ruined neck.

### E25-006 | The Editor's Necessary Knife | 1867-04

The journal's editor returned my proofs with the title altered and the phrase 'the soul's mechanism' struck from the heading. A note beside the offending words read, 'conjecture, not result.' I wrote half a furious refusal. Heller asked whether I had actually observed a soul operating in any preparation listed in the paper. I had not, and resented his evident enjoyment of the answer. The rewritten version restored the figures to their proper position and confined my speculations about artificial organization to the final section. The editor accepted it. I told Henry that I had instructed the journal how best to arrange an idea, and Heller, standing in the doorway, did not correct me. His silence was a cruelty of a refined kind. Yet the paper would have been indefensible without those cuts. There are times when the knife is a greater friend to the writer than the wreath. I preferred the wreath, of course.

### E10-011 | The Borrowed Royal Title | 1867-05

Henry asked why I called the first creature a Queen when she possessed no subjects. I answered that royalty preceded its court in numerous European histories. Clara suggested that I wanted her disobedience to look ceremonial. Henry smiled too readily. To settle the matter I placed a small paper throne in the vessel and watched the Queen disassemble one arm of it. I was delighted, an inconvenient admission since my hypothesis had been insulted in miniature. Henry took the broken arm home to sketch its joinery and returned it with a note about leverage. I kept the note and not the throne. A name can be an observation, a hope, or a demand imposed upon something that has not agreed to bear it. I persisted in calling her Majesty. She continued to behave as if the title conferred no obligation whatever.

### E13-009 | The Queen and the Velvet | 1867-05

When Leonie first saw the Queen she did not exclaim over the miracle. She asked why the platform was draped in velvet when the creature repeatedly sought the uncovered edge. I explained that velvet lent dignity and protected her from reflected glare. Leonie placed a narrow strip of plain linen beside the purple fold and withdrew her hand. The Queen approached the linen and sat with her back to my arrangement. Clara advised us to repeat the observation under different light. We did so. The result was irritatingly consistent. Leonie removed the velvet and asked what dignity was worth to a being who could neither purchase it nor consent to its price. I answered that beauty mattered even when its intended audience was ungrateful. The Queen then tugged a thread free and examined it more attentively than she had ever examined me. I recorded that the linen had displaced an aesthetic preference. I did not specify whose preference. Leonie noticed, but did not insist on a correction.

### E19-007 | Velvet Does Not Make a Sovereign | 1867-05

Leonie brought a remnant of violet velvet from a theatre costume. I cut a seat for the Queen and had it fitted beneath her favored ledge. The little monarch approached, placed one hand upon the cloth and retreated with unmistakable dispatch. I blamed the dye. Clara blamed my conceit. Leonie, who had pinned costumes onto living performers for years, suggested that the nap scratched. I touched it with my finger and felt nothing. She put the smallest splinter of thread against the sensitive skin of my wrist. I felt it perfectly then. We replaced the velvet with a plain strip of polished wood and the Queen returned. I kept the scrap in an envelope labelled REGALIA REJECTED. It amused me to imagine a monarch whose taste could not be bought, even with fabric of a most expensive shade.

### E25-007 | The Signed Offprint | 1867-05

The printed offprints arrived bound in twine. There was paste on the cover, a small error in a heading, and the faint smell of wet paper. It was not the noble sensation I had expected from immortality by typography. Then a visiting scholar asked me to inscribe his copy. I laughed at the childishness of autographs and took exquisite care not to blot mine. Kappel received a copy with an inscription more formal than our usual conversation. I left another, quite casually, beside the faculty visitor's register. Lenz wanted to know who had paid for the additional impressions. That afternoon I ate a peach on the staircase and stained the cuff I had put on for the occasion. I was much less troubled by that mark than I might have been the previous year. The paper bore my name where another man could read it. This, I discovered, was a pleasure not fully provided by the discovery itself.

### E02-015 | A King with an Inconvenient Heart | 1867-06

The second creature emerged with the complacent features of Henry VIII, a resemblance which I confess gave me disproportionate delight. He fastened his attention on the Queen and neglected his food when her vessel was removed. I called it attachment. Clara called it distress. Neither of us possessed an instrument capable of settling the matter, but I had one capable of ending the knocking against the glass: I restored the Queen to his sight. He ate. There was a vulgar triumph in being obeyed by a king, although I did not then appreciate which party had issued the command. I separated their vessels less often afterward. A mere convenience, I told visitors, as though I had not rearranged half the bench to accommodate a creature smaller than my thumb.

### E25-008 | Two Contrary Letters | 1867-06

Two letters arrived at breakfast. One announced that my paper had proved the immortality of the soul; the other accused me of proving its extinction. I placed them on opposite sides of my plate while I ate an egg boiled to a consistency which might have served as a new mineral. Neither correspondent had read the conclusion carefully. I drafted replies with equal severity, then softened both, for even those who misunderstand a man may invite him to speak. A shorter notice disagreed with my account of reflexes and quoted the figures accurately. It was the best of the three responses. Clara asked whether I would allow a critic to inspect all my preparations. I said he might see those suitable for public inspection. She noticed the adjective. We quarrelled until the kettle boiled dry, then laughed without resolving anything. It was an excellent week. People had begun to disagree with me in distant rooms. I could almost hear the arguments crossing Europe.

### E13-010 | The Seat Beside the King | 1867-07

Henry spent a disagreeable afternoon measuring the distance the King travelled toward the Queen when her jar was brought near. He proposed that attraction might be a response to movement or warmth. I suggested that the King possessed a taste for the unattainable, which made him the least original monarch in history. Leonie had been asked to sew a curtain for the testing alcove. She watched us move the jars about as though arranging a marriage between porcelain figures, then asked what evidence would persuade us that the King wished for something else. Henry answered at once. I offered a more elaborate answer and took longer. We repeated the trial with an empty jar and discovered, to my amusement, that the King approached the motion even without his sovereign. The result did not extinguish my romantic explanation. It merely made it less sufficient. I preserved both in the ledger, one in ink and one in the margin, where a candid man might have reversed their positions.

### E19-008 | The King's Nose | 1867-07

I compared the King's face with an engraving of Henry VIII, then with a wax bust borrowed from a collector whose trust in my scholarship deserved a better fate. From the left, the resemblance was delicious. From the right, he might have been a provincial tax official with a very small grievance. Henry said I had chosen the Tudor king because my miniature already disapproved of being contradicted. Lenz proposed that we rotate the engraving until history behaved. I laughed more than the joke warranted, then measured the profile again. The numbers refused my preferred comparison. In the ledger I wrote that the resemblance was evocative, not diagnostic. At dinner I told the story as though the King himself had selected the model. Scholars must sometimes be permitted a little poetic licence after correcting their own diagrams.

### E25-009 | A Place Card in Munich | 1867-07

They seated me beneath a portrait whose owner was distinguished chiefly by the expense of his frame. At the table sat two physicians, clergymen of different persuasions, an enthusiast for spiritual inquiry, and a woman who knew rather more about instruments than the men who had introduced her. The room smelled of candle smoke and rain-soaked wool. I was invited to explain the paper, and the questions came with gratifying speed. The woman asked whether an investigator who produced a living subject would thereby possess a claim upon it. I disliked 'possess' and made an elegant little joke about the vocabulary of solicitors. Several men laughed. She repeated the question without the objectionable word. I did not answer it any better. At the end of the evening a visitor offered an introduction in Paris during the gathering of physicians. I pocketed the place card bearing my name in a stranger's fine handwriting. It was a more precious souvenir than my arguments deserved.

### E02-016 | The Archbishop's Objection | 1867-08

A third little figure developed in an attitude of disapproval so sustained that we named him Archbishop before his clothing had properly formed. He interrupted the King's displays by striking a tiny staff against the base of his vessel. Lenz, who found this uproariously funny, positioned the jars so that the King could see both the Queen and the Archbishop. The result was a three-sided quarrel conducted with gestures too small to hear. I intended to study hierarchy and instead spent the morning shifting glass to prevent injury. Clara pointed out that the Archbishop did not object to the Queen at all, only to the King approaching her. I had assigned a theological motive to an observation that did not require one. The distinction displeased me because it was interesting.

### E16-009 | A Pamphlet Read Twice | 1867-08

I read one of Karl Heinrich Ulrichs's tracts in a Munich printer's waiting room in 1867. Its arguments about men whose affections turned toward men were expressed with a lawyer's appetite for categories, several of which I found biologically unpersuasive. Yet the candor of the enterprise was remarkable. He did not petition for a toleration disguised as illness; he demanded that the law explain its own jurisdiction. I made notes in the margin, then tore them out because I had written upon a borrowed copy. A printer's assistant asked whether I knew the author's work. I said that I knew an argument when I saw one, regardless of its author's domestic arrangements. It was a clever answer and a cowardly one, a combination in which I had lately become proficient. I bought another copy before leaving. I did not meet Ulrichs and have no right to borrow his courage for my own account. His printed words did, however, make a question I had considered private appear on a public page.

### E25-010 | The City with No Obligations | 1867-08-15

Paris did not announce my arrival. Roofs, yards and advertisements passed the train window with an indifference that ought to have been insulting, but which I found strangely liberating. I had a programme, two copies of the paper and a dark coat protected by cardboard. The porter treated the offprints as ordinary paper. From my narrow hotel room I heard carriage wheels and a woman calling a name I could not distinguish. The streets were crowded with men who had brought inventions to the Exposition and hoped someone important would look at them. I was one such man, although my invention was chiefly an argument. I dined alone under a spotted mirror and found I had no student to correct, no faculty door to open, no experiment requiring an account before morning. I paid too much for the fish and entered the sum in my ledger. It was one of the pleasantest evenings of my youth. I did not need an audience every hour to enjoy being important.

### E25-011 | The Objection Worth Keeping | 1867-08-17

A man introduced me at an informal gathering near the physicians' congress as though I had already enclosed immortality in a glass bottle. I corrected him, partly from conscience and partly because such a description rendered my achievement vulgar. I had no apparatus and claimed no official demonstration. The offprints supplied the evening's entire machinery. A French physician praised my observations on declining muscular response, then asked whether repeated stimulation had itself altered the preparation. For three minutes I disliked him. Afterward I wrote the objection into my notebook before the compliments, though I entered those also. Two other gentlemen wished to claim my work for opposite schools of metaphysics. Between them I felt almost rich enough to agree with both. When I returned to the hotel my cuff smelled of tobacco and my pocket contained three addresses. I had met scholars who thought me wrong without wishing me diminished. There was more pleasure in it than I had expected.

### E25-012 | The Correspondent's Hand | 1867-08-20

Monsieur Jolivet had actually read my paper, including the dull table of failed responses. We began discussing the difference between movement and purpose. He spoke quickly and clearly, and his pencil paused before a question as if the question mattered to him. He apologised for arriving late; within the hour I had entirely forgotten we had arranged a finite interview. His cuff bore a grey pencil mark. When he moved his chair closer to inspect a figure, I observed his hand upon the table more closely than the diagram between us. I called this pleasure intellectual sympathy. Theodor had left the previous autumn and I knew enough not to inquire very closely into the distinction. We ordered a second coffee. Jolivet caught a mistaken label in my illustration, and, unlike several colleagues, did not perform triumph at discovering it. I found him extraordinarily agreeable. I knew he was conducting an interview. I did not take the precaution of remembering it at every moment.

### E25-013 | What Belongs to the Maker | 1867-08-20

Jolivet asked what I should do with the soul if I found it. Understand it, I answered. And afterwards? If a principle is understood, I said, the next question is whether it can be reproduced. He did not recoil. He asked instead whether the resulting creature would belong to the experimenter. I objected to the language of property, and he reminded me that I had first spoken of production. This was clever and not entirely fair, which is to say that it was fairer than I liked. We discussed surgeons, necessity, patients unable to consent, and the separate obligations of an investigation undertaken for knowledge. I gave a splendid answer concerning timid regulations. He asked whether my answer applied equally to a creature I had myself called into being. I stirred undissolved sugar until the spoon rang against the porcelain. The argument had become less theoretical than I wished to admit. Jolivet wrote a phrase in his notebook. I did not ask which. I left persuaded I had won the exchange, although I could no longer recall the proposition we had disputed.

### E25-014 | A Sentence I Could Not Correct | 1867-08-28

The newspaper arrived folded at the scientific feature. I read the opening with satisfaction. It praised the technical caution of my memoir and refused to credit claims I had never made. At the window, where the print was clearer, I reached the passage about constructing the conditions of life. Jolivet had retained my words with painful fidelity. The trouble lay not in fabrication but in his arrangement of questions. I had sounded rather less humane than I had intended, especially when speaking of future subjects' rights. Near the end he suggested that my will had not yet learned to govern itself. I laughed aloud. A fellow lodger did not. I wrote home that an excellent French newspaper had devoted considerable space to my work. This was quite true. I omitted the article's actual judgment. Even then, with humiliation beginning to kindle, there was real pleasure in seeing my name in so large a column. The two feelings did not cancel each other.

### E10-012 | The Servant Who Saw Too Much | 1867-09

A porter found a broken crate bearing the markings of Lenz's workshop and asked what sort of glass required so much straw and such nervous instructions. Kappel told him it was fragile research material. The porter asked whether fragile research material ordinarily seemed to be knocking at its own box. The sound was a loose wooden cleat, as we discovered when the crate was opened; the story had already acquired a heartbeat by the time it reached the porter's friends. I was furious that a stupid accident should be more persuasive than three years of careful work. Kappel proposed admitting a neutral observer to dispel suspicion. I refused. Each alternative offended me differently: secrecy encouraged legend, publicity endangered control. I chose control. When the story reached the faculty corridor, the crate had acquired a second knock and a small creature that I had never grown.

### E19-009 | The Archbishop's Unanswered Prayer | 1867-09

I set a minute altar near the Archbishop's chamber, an exquisite piece of folded paper, and rang a silver bell while he stood before it. He did not bow. He did not cross himself. He went very still when the bell sounded, and afterward moved toward the place from which the vibration came. I said he was attending to a summons. Clara asked whose summons, God's or mine. I told her those were not invariably distinct in a laboratory. She declined to laugh. We altered the interval of the sound and moved the altar, which remained of no interest whatever. I entered his response under SOUND, not DEVOTION. The little altar stayed on a shelf for months. Its uselessness offended me, because the folds were more elegant than the majority of churches I knew.

### E25-015 | The Correction Which Was Not an Apology | 1867-09

I wrote to Jolivet demanding that he distinguish the proposed reproduction of organized life from any claim that I had accomplished it. He replied with courtesy and pointed to the very paragraph in which he had already made that distinction. There was nothing to correct. I held the letter near the lamp, where the watermark showed through, searching for an insolent expression. I found none. I wrote a second letter about his insinuations concerning responsibility, one whose severity would have done honor to an unhappy prosecutor. It remained on my desk unsent. What I wanted, as I knew even then, was not merely a correction in the newspaper. I wanted another of those unhurried conversations in which he had seemed to find me amusing and important. The letter requested no such thing. At last I put his answer among my offprints. I told myself I had preserved the evidence of his journalistic cunning. I had also preserved his handwriting.

### E25-016 | The Invitation Withdrawn | 1867-09

The proposed lecture had been arranged by a gentleman who wrote of natural philosophy in one paragraph and of his parish in the next. His second letter arrived with a courtly regret: members of the society had become uncertain whether my paper affirmed the immortality of the soul. I had affirmed nothing of the kind and had spent a considerable sum explaining that exact point. I announced at supper that I did not require the approval of any gathering afraid of its own questions. Clara asked whether I had been looking forward to the lecture. I said, 'Naturally not,' with such force that she passed me the salt instead of answering. In my pocket was the timetable for the journey I should no longer make. I kept it until the paper wore through at its fold. The lost invitation was not the beginning of a universal prohibition. Two other men still wrote to me about the measurements. The absence of a particular room troubled me because I had already pictured myself standing at its centre.

### E02-017 | The Man in the Black Cape | 1867-10

I had not intended to cultivate anything resembling myself. Nonetheless the fourth figure emerged lean, dark and rather theatrical, with a black cape whose folds appeared before the rest of his costume had assumed proper definition. Lenz called him the Devil. Henry, shown the figure during one carefully supervised visit, regarded him with more unease than he had shown at the Queen. The Devil bowed toward his reflection in the glass and then turned his back upon us. I was delighted. Henry asked whether the miniature had learned vanity from its maker. I told him that resemblance proved kinship no more reliably than a portrait proved paternity. Yet I moved the little mirror out of the laboratory afterward. Certain jokes are more uncomfortable when they resemble observations.

### E25-017 | The Rumour Finds a Vocabulary | 1867-10

A crate from Lenz's workshop gave out a knocking sound when a porter lifted it. The noise came from a loose cleat, but by the time the tale reached the faculty it involved a small beating heart. Kappel proposed allowing someone unconnected with the experiments to examine the room. I refused. The private work was not ready, and no journalist had earned the right to inspect it merely by putting me in print. Kappel asked why I was arguing with him as though he were the reporter. I answered that the distinction between them had lately grown thin. This was unjust. I knew it while speaking. In the corridor a younger scholar remarked upon my opinion that nature might be constructed. He had not read the journal, only the newspaper. I responded with a lecture on second-hand knowledge and watched him withdraw. The crate had done more immediate harm than the article. Yet the article had taught those hearing the crate what sort of story to expect. I would later pretend the two matters formed a single conspiracy. They had scarcely needed to cooperate.

### E10-013 | The Second Set of Figures | 1867-11

I prepared one table for scientific use and another for a prospective patron, removing the damaged observations from the second because they would require more explanation than the fellow could bear. Henry discovered the discrepancy and asked whether a result made prettier for money remained a result. I replied that I had omitted noise, not evidence. He laid the two sheets side by side and pointed to a small irregularity that appeared in three of the supposedly noisy readings. Clara examined it, then asked for the original notebooks. We reinstated the figures. The patron saw both versions and declined to support us. I cursed Henry's delicacy and Clara's stubbornness. A fortnight later the irregularity helped us detect a recurrent fault in the apparatus. I sent Henry a copy of the revised table without comment. He wrote 'I see it' on the back. I have retained that scrap longer than the patron's name.

### E13-011 | The Crown Nobody Asked For | 1867-11

Leonie produced a crown small enough for the Queen, using fine wire and a red glass bead Lenz had rejected as imperfect. It was beautifully made. I rewarded the gift with an inventory of its historical errors, then carried it to the laboratory in a cotton-lined box. The Queen stood beneath it for several moments without attempting to take it. When I opened the cabinet the following morning, the crown lay behind a feeding dish. I accused Lenz of having brushed it aside. He showed me where the Queen had dragged it herself, leaving a trail in the damp medium. Leonie laughed when I told her. She asked whether the Queen was a more discerning patron than I. I said she had the same intolerable inclination to judge a gift by whether she wanted it. Nevertheless, before returning the crown to its box I repaired the bent wire myself. I took uncommon care with a thing its intended owner appeared to dislike.

### E19-010 | The Devil's Reflection | 1867-11

The Devil came to his little cape by means of Leonie's sewing, not infernal providence. She insisted that the shoulders must fall correctly if the garment was to be seen through glass. I held a polished spoon beside my own face and challenged Henry to name the likeness. He said the creature seemed less inclined than I to interrupt a companion. I found this unscientific, and may have been less gracious about the observation than my confidence deserved. The little gentleman paused before his reflection, examined it and walked away. Leonie suggested he had better things to do than admire his own face. I put the spoon in a drawer and explained that its curvature rendered it useless as an instrument. I used it thereafter to stir my gin, where inaccuracies of proportion were rather welcome.

### E02-018 | Music Before Supper | 1867-12

The Ballerina responded to Mendelssohn's 'Spring Song' and to very little else we tried. Clara first played the phrase on an ill-tuned piano in the room below, and the tiny dancer rose from her resting place. When the tune stopped, so did she. I assumed any melody in the same tempo would serve; she refused three substitutions, one played so badly that I sympathized with her judgment. A week later Clara was delayed, and I found myself humming at the jar while stirring soup. The Ballerina did not move. I ate the soup cold and waited for Clara's return. This dependence on a particular piece of music made the laboratory less controllable, and, to my surprise, considerably more pleasant.

### E25-018 | Good Company without a Defence | 1867-12

Clara brought bread to the laboratory and discovered me composing another answer to a clerical critic. She read the first paragraph, set the bread beside my elbow, and said that I might finish my reply after eating. Lenz was repairing a fine joint in the neighbouring room; the flame made a brief blue column which threw our shadows against the door. We spoke not of newspapers but of a misbehaving specimen, the price of coal, and the Ballerina's extraordinary preference for one melody. I was laughing when I noticed that nobody at the table required me to justify the study of life. I had begun to think that every conversation must turn into a hearing. Clara asked whether Paris had been pleasant. I said that it had been, until certain persons discovered the profit to be made from clever questions. She did not pursue the matter. After they left I opened the article at its first paragraph and read only the praise. There are evenings on which one may choose a smaller injury. I had not yet learned the difference between forgiveness and postponement.

### E13-012 | The Room Before the Committee | 1868-01

When word came that the faculty might ask to inspect the laboratory, I spent an evening placing the vessels in a configuration that would compel attention. The Queen stood at the center, the Archbishop to one side and the King opposite, as though judgment and appetite were offices of state. Leonie arrived to repair a curtain and said the arrangement made the little people resemble evidence staged for a trial whose outcome I had already decided. Kappel recommended that I place the ordinary observation books at the front. I refused because their inconsistent figures would distract from the central achievement. Leonie pushed a lamp two inches left and transformed the composition so completely that I allowed her to finish. Then, when both visitors had gone, I put the irregular books beneath the table. I have never found it difficult to tell when others are concealing facts. With myself, naturally, the distinction between selection and concealment requires a finer instrument.

### E19-011 | The Ballerina Refuses an Encore | 1868-01

I had arranged for the Ballerina's favored music to be played three times to impress a private guest. She danced through the first rendition, paused before the conclusion of the second, and refused altogether to respond to the third. I blamed the pianist, who was not present. Leonie played the melody softly once more after an hour of silence, and the tiny dancer raised one arm before sitting down. Leonie asked what would happen to a human dancer obliged to repeat the same figure without rest until an audience was persuaded of its existence. I said we were discussing a creation, not a theatre contract. Yet I sent my guest away and shortened the subsequent sessions. I still liked an encore. I found I liked it better when it was offered rather than exacted.

### E02-019 | Salt in the Water | 1868-02

The seaweed arrived leaking brine through three layers of paper and a courier's protest. From it, by means I shall not publish, came the Mermaid. She occupied her watery chamber in such perfect stillness that I feared a beautiful failure. Clara discovered that she moved when visitors departed. We watched from behind a screen, uncomfortably bent like burglars in our own laboratory, while the little figure rose and vanished among the weed. I wrote 'timid' in the ledger. Later I drew a line through it and added 'reacts to observers.' I have not destroyed the first word. ### E10-014 | The Private Invitation | 1868-03

Heller came privately rather than sending for me. He had heard descriptions of the miniature beings and wished to see evidence before the faculty was obliged to act upon rumor. Henry urged me to admit him. Clara said the conditions could be arranged if Heller agreed to observe without disturbing the vessels. I prepared three drafts of an invitation. Each seemed to surrender something to a man who might afterwards vote upon my work. In the end I showed Heller selected records and declined to open the inner room. He examined the curves and asked whether the living forms could choose not to be examined. I called this a moral question smuggled into a technical visit. He said technical questions frequently acquire occupants. We parted without an agreement. I still wonder whether a demonstration would have altered his conduct. That is not the same as wishing I had given him one.

### E19-012 | The Cabinet of Flawless Things | 1868-03

Reuter found a drawer lined in blue felt. It contained the things I had spared the wastebasket because they were handsome: a transparent shell, a minute symmetrical form that had never responded, and a delicate branching structure that suggested intelligence only if one wished it. He asked where I had put their dates and controls. I was furious at the implication that beauty had no probative force. Then I discovered I could not remember which of two nearly identical specimens had been kept warm. I took my pen and labelled the drawer AESTHETIC, NOT EVIDENCE, before he could do it for me. He looked disappointed rather than triumphant. We closed the drawer. It remains the only collection in my laboratory for which I refuse to manufacture a scientific justification.

### E25-019 | The Newspaper on the Committee's Table | 1868-03

Heller came to examine matters which a philosopher should not have needed to explain under oath. The committee wanted records of apparatus, the supervision of assistants, and the safety of certain investigations whose nature had already inspired more rumour than fact. When I entered the waiting room the French clipping lay among the papers. I knew the page before I could read the type, for one corner had torn beside Jolivet's last paragraph. I asked whether an article by a journalist constituted a scientific accusation. Heller said that it did not, and moved it aside. The questions that followed concerned invoices, access to the workrooms, and decisions I had taken without adequate consultation. Several were fair. I answered all of them as though they were variations of the question about my soul. Afterward I recalled Heller's hand pushing the newspaper out of the centre of the table. At the time I took the gesture as proof that the proceedings were absurd. In truth, he had been showing me where the actual difficulty lay.

### E02-020 | The Visit from the Committee | 1868-04

The committee arrived with the solemnity of undertakers pricing a living man. Its charges were conduct unbecoming my office and experiments injurious to the moral fabric of the university, a textile evidently too delicate to survive a six-inch vessel. I offered observations and withheld my method. Heller read the pages while two others inspected my face as if the proof of corruption might be found there. A younger member asked if the little beings suffered. I spoke on the difficulty of defining suffering, with all the authority I could assemble. The question remained unanswered. Henry waited outside and said afterward that my discourse had done us no good. I reminded him that a good answer and a useful answer are not necessarily the same. He looked less comforted than I had intended.

### E13-013 | The Joke in the Corridor | 1868-05

Heller passed me outside the committee room without returning my greeting. Several junior scholars stood nearby pretending to examine a noticeboard. I said, distinctly enough for all to hear, that the faculty had at last discovered a form of life it could create without my assistance: the administrative parasite. Someone laughed, too quickly and then not at all. Leonie had come to retrieve a garment from my rooms and overheard the exchange. She told me afterward that the joke was clever and had cost me the last possibility of a sympathetic hearing. I answered that a sympathetic hearing obtained through silence was merely a conviction postponed. She said there were times when preserving one's options was not cowardice. I began a reply, decided it would require more than the corridor deserved, and went upstairs to pack the samples. The same young scholar who laughed later refused to meet my eyes. I remember his embarrassment more sharply than Heller's silence.

### E19-013 | The List I Did Not Submit | 1868-05

While the faculty prepared its questions I made an inventory of every instrument, culture, invoice and living vessel in my care. It grew into an indictment of the institution, including the names of men and women whose labor had permitted my work. Lenz delivered a broken clamp and warned that an inquiry could travel along a receipt as easily as an accusation. Clara said that my witnesses had not all volunteered to become defendants. I drew a line through several suppliers and removed the pages concerning arrangements never approved by any committee. Whether this was loyalty, vanity or simple prudence depends upon who tells the story. I placed the full inventory in a sealed envelope. My shorter reply did not save my chair. It did not condemn everyone who had sat near it either.

### E10-015 | A Request for Reconsideration | 1868-06

The committee had heard its witnesses by June, though it had not yet pronounced sentence. I was allowed to submit a statement of reconsideration. Kappel brought me the official form, two pages of ruled paper, and advice to say that the observations were incomplete and the circumstances regrettable. I wrote that they were incomplete because life itself was not finished and that regret would not improve a measurement. Heller, who had come to collect another document, read the page with a tired expression. He said that I had once been capable of winning an argument without forcing an enemy to lose his dignity. I asked whether the committee had extended me that courtesy. He did not answer. I filed the statement. For many years I told the story as one of heroic refusal. Sometimes I remember the unused sheet Kappel had left beneath my hand, and that I might have written something else without falsifying a single fact.

### E19-014 | A Number Removed | 1868-06

Reuter found an arithmetic error in my summary of survival intervals, and a particularly cruel one, for it improved the apparent outcome precisely where the committee wished to see it. I could have corrected the working papers quietly, leaving the summary untouched. Instead I took a knife to the number and rewrote the page. Clara, waiting in the corridor, said that no one on the committee would have checked it. I replied that I might have. This was not a declaration of virtue. I was furious that an error of my own had provided the institution with an argument it did not deserve. The new figure made no difference to the verdict. I still remember the scraping of the knife and the clock that continued ticking while I debated whether a single honest number could cost a man his laboratory.

### E02-021 | The Dismissal and the Box | 1868-07

The faculty's sentence arrived in a thick envelope bearing very little argument and a great quantity of institutional dignity. I was dismissed. Heller was absent when the matter was concluded. Kappel brought a crate lined with discarded examination papers, an arrangement which pleased me for reasons I did not care to explain. A clerk showed me a torn draft of a letter in which Henry had offered to speak in my defense. The letter had not been sent. I might have asked him why; I preferred, at the time, to despise his hesitation without hearing its cause. Clara insisted that the living vessels be carried upright. I carried my blue ledger. That evening I possessed neither laboratory nor professorship, although no committee in creation could divest me of the learning by which I had obtained them.

### E25-020 | The Sentence with Other Causes | 1868-07

The dismissal arrived in a thick official envelope. I knew the language before I had reached the signature: conduct, confidence, propriety, conditions of inquiry. Not one clause named Jolivet. I nevertheless thought of his quiet questions, and in my anger constructed a path from the café to this room as straight as a surveyor's line. Clara came to see that the living vessels travelled upright. Kappel brought a crate and said very little. I tried to make a joke about how the university had finally learned to produce a ghost, meaning a professor who remained after his chair had been removed. Nobody laughed. In the weeks that followed I named the article among the reasons I had been wronged. I did not name the locks, the refusals to admit witnesses, the injured assistants, or the documents in which I had preferred elegance to accountability. Those matters did not disappear because I could arrange my grievances in a more pleasing order. I took the blue ledger away and left the doors to others.

### E13-014 | The Coat with an Unfinished Hem | 1868-08

I collected the last of my clothes from Leonie's workroom after the dismissal. My black coat hung from a nail with its hem still pinned. She said she had not finished the alteration because I had failed to arrive for the fitting on three occasions. I reminded her that my professional circumstances had become unusually complicated. She took out the pins, laid the coat upon the table, and said that a man who refused to stand still could hardly demand perfect tailoring. Clara arrived with clean linen for the tower. For a moment the two women discussed weather and mending as though my expulsion were a minor inconvenience to ordinary life. I found this intolerable. I paid Leonie in cash, although she had not asked, and left before either could offer condolences. At the tower that evening I discovered one pin remaining in the lining. I wore the coat for weeks with the point against my wrist rather than take it back. There is a difference between endurance and stupidity. On that occasion I probably crossed it.

### E19-015 | The One Vessel Left Behind | 1868-08

There was room for one more vessel in the wagon. I had to choose between an inactive specimen in an exceptionally handsome bowl and the crate of spare stoppers Lenz insisted we would need before the winter. I chose the stoppers and announced the decision as a triumph of reason over attachment. Lenz did not look at me. He tied the crate with wet rope while I returned alone to the annex. On the bench a chalk circle showed where the bowl had stood for months. I copied the label into the blue ledger, although the last observations were several weeks old, and left the vessel where the faculty might take possession of it. I have occasionally imagined someone opening it years afterward. I have no evidence that anyone did. The empty circle was a more exact memory than any speech I prepared for that day.

### E03-001 | The Tower with Three Dry Rooms | 1868-09

The tower was cheaper than any respectable laboratory and possessed three rooms that remained dry in rain, a virtue sufficient to outweigh its missing stair rail. Lenz tested the upper floor with his boot and refused to carry a jar across it until the beams had been supported. I called his caution provincial. He asked whether I preferred the Queen to arrive on the ground floor by gravity. Clara brought straw, blankets and two pails whose contents were not to be confused. We moved the creatures before the books, the books before my clothes. At dusk the Queen was settled in the least draughty corner, and I discovered that my own bed was damp. I slept beside the heating stove and congratulated myself on having chosen independence.

### E25-021 | The Article beside the Window | 1868-10

The tower contained three rooms that remained reliably dry and many that did not. I placed the French newspaper beneath a stack of discarded proofs so the draft from the window would not carry it across the floor. Then I moved it to a drawer, then returned it to the table because I could not bear to have it mistaken for waste. Rain made a little channel under the outer door. The paper had acquired a stain at one corner and smelled of dust when opened. I attempted to write a corrected account of the interview, putting my finest arguments into answers I had not delivered. By midnight I had produced a conversation in which Jolivet was both humiliated and grateful. I knew it was an invention. I preserved the draft for an evening, then tore it into strips for the stove. The published account remained. I preferred my adversary dishonest; it was intolerably inconvenient that his questions had been good.

### E03-002 | The Price of Coal | 1868-11

At the university, coal had entered the cellar by an arrangement as natural and inexplicable as Providence. At the tower it came wet, late, and at a price which might have warmed us by outrage alone. I economized on heat until the King lost appetite and the Queen grew languid. Clara found a box of my cigars and laid it beside the fuel bill. She was offensively silent. I disposed of the cigars and bought coal. The creatures revived, though I shall not pretend that one evening establishes a complete theory of temperature. I recorded the readings beside the expense and put the empty cigar box away. For a week afterward I found myself seeking it at the usual hour. Habit is a petty tyrant, with none of the grandeur of an emperor.

## 1869

### E11-001 | The Coat on the Nail | 1869-01

My professorial coat hung from a nail in the tower's stairwell because the damp made a wardrobe an architectural joke. I had worn it during the hearing and would not relinquish it merely because the university had reclaimed its keys. Clara found the lining spotted with mildew. She said she would have it dried and patched; I said the coat was perfectly serviceable. Lenz pointed out that the shoulders had begun to smell like a neglected cellar. I gave it over with a ceremony of offended dignity. Clara returned it three days later, the cuffs repaired with thread slightly darker than the original. At a time when every other article seemed to have changed its address or its owner, I found the coat in the same place each morning. I called this discipline. Clara called it a curious attachment to a poor nail. Neither description accounts for the fact that I put it on before answering official letters.

### E03-003 | The Door That Would Not Lock | 1869-02

Someone tried the tower door during a night of sleet. I heard the latch rise twice, then a boot on the step. By the time I reached the entrance no one was there. Lenz examined the lock the following morning and found it damaged by rust rather than an intruder. I disliked this explanation, for it denied me a suitably sinister enemy. He fitted a new bolt and asked why I kept a lamp burning against the window. I told him it allowed me to observe the tower's surroundings. He said it also advertised that someone was awake inside. We moved the light and installed a small shaded lantern. I did not abandon my suspicion, but for the next week I listened more carefully before naming every sound a persecutor.

### E22-001 | The Coal Woman's Measure | 1869-03

Rosa Brandl arrived with coal in a cart that shed black water over my threshold. I asked her to place three baskets beneath the east stairs and she declined until she had inspected the floor. It was rotten, she said, and she would not have the collapse of an aristocratic ruin attributed to her horse. I informed her that the place possessed no aristocracy except the intellectual kind. She placed the coal in the kitchen instead, where its dust settled upon my clean papers. Her measure was tin, deeply dented, and indisputably honest. I paid more than I had intended, then watched her carry the final basket without the assistance I had neglected to offer. For three winters afterward she knew my fuel expenditure more accurately than any surviving colleague knew my work. She never called me Professor. Curiously, I did not correct her.

### E03-004 | A Letter from Henry | 1869-05

Henry wrote from somewhere beyond my immediate reach. His letter asked after the smaller creatures and whether I had found a way to maintain viable tissue at greater scale. It contained no offer to visit. I began a reply of forty pages and reduced it to four. I sent him observations on the limits of growth but refused the procedure he sought, although I could scarcely have given him one. At the end I added a line asking whether he had recovered from the quarrels of our final university months. I struck it out and wrote instead that the Queen continued to exhibit surprising preferences. He did not answer for several years. I kept his letter under the blue ledger where it left a pale rectangular mark.

### E11-002 | A Letter without a Title | 1869-07

Henry's letter had arrived in May. I had answered the technical question it contained within a week and spent another six drafting a second letter that would make him understand the larger offense done to me. Kappel visited with new paper and asked whom I intended the second document to persuade. I said Henry deserved to know how the university had punished success. Kappel asked why I had written the word 'witness' four times. I crossed it out three times and sent only the technical correction. I have never discovered whether Henry received that answer. The longer letter remained in the drawer beside his youthful anatomical sketches. I preferred to consider the correspondence an unfinished scientific exchange. The drafts suggested another possibility, one so tiresomely personal that I bound them together and labelled the packet 'administration.'

### E03-005 | The King Takes a Fall | 1869-08

The King fell from his platform in the course of some performance meant, I presume, to impress the Queen. His gait afterward was slow. I declared that a fall of such trifling proportions could do no harm, a confident statement pronounced before I had examined him. Clara moved the furnishings nearer the floor while I prepared reasons why the original height had been sensible. The Queen remained on the side of her vessel facing him until he recovered. I shall not make a romance of it. The King promptly attempted to scale our new partition and behaved as though safety were an indignity inflicted by lesser courtiers. I almost applauded his spirit. Then I adjusted the barrier and avoided looking at Clara.

### E22-002 | A Window for the Western Jar | 1869-08

I liked the western light upon the Queen's glass. It made the little walls seem part of a palace. Clara liked neither the light nor the temperature; she touched a pane and withdrew her finger with a perfectly unnecessary expression of victory. One occupant had begun avoiding the exposed side. I suggested shyness, a quality to which miniature royalty is as entitled as the ordinary sort. Clara hung a piece of washed linen over the window and waited while I made the measurements again. They improved. The Queen moved about her chamber with a composure I preferred to attribute to restored court etiquette. For weeks I raised the shade before visitors arrived and lowered it as they departed. Clara discovered the practice when she came unexpectedly for the account book. I told her the exhibition was essential to patronage. She left the curtain cord in my hand and made no reply. It was a remarkably effective form of discourtesy.

### E11-003 | A Debt in Small Coins | 1869-11

Lenz came to fit a glass valve and found me counting copper coins beside an untouched dish of potatoes. The university stipend had ceased. Two patrons who promised sympathy had failed to send payment. He set the valve in place and gave me a bill with the date left open. I objected that I could pay for honest work. He replied that he knew this, which was why he was willing to wait. Clara arrived later with coal and an account she refused to describe as charity. I insisted upon signing every debt in the blue ledger. They signed as witnesses, rather as though I were founding a bank. I repaid the sums, not always promptly. Years later when younger men proposed arrangements based solely upon mutual enthusiasm, I demanded a date, a price, and a means of leaving. Trust had reached me through a bill that nobody was in a hurry to collect.

### E25-022 | No Letter Sent to Paris | 1869-11

A year after my removal I found an envelope addressed to Jolivet behind a book of anatomical plates. I had filled it with notes on a new test of excitability, thinking I might reopen correspondence on grounds that would be beyond personal misunderstanding. The address was accurate but the envelope had no stamp. I held it beside the stove while the glass warmed my other hand. The question was absurdly simple: did I wish to send him a scientific correction, or did I wish him to remember me? I did not settle it. I laid the notes in the blue ledger and put the envelope into the drawer where I kept receipts too important to discard and too unimportant to display. I had begun telling acquaintances that journalists were natural parasites upon genius. Yet I knew perfectly well that he had read every page I showed him. If a parasite could ask a better question than a professor, the definition required some attention. I let the envelope stay unstamped.

### E13-015 | A Letter with No Performance | 1869-12

Leonie wrote to ask whether I had finally found a tailor nearer the tower. Beneath the question she noted that the municipal hall had replaced its old floorboards, rendering our first disagreement obsolete. I drafted a reply discussing the acoustical disadvantages of the replacement timber and the intellectual inadequacy of the faculty which had once used the hall. I did not send it. Instead I enclosed the coat's remaining pin in a folded paper and wrote: You were right about this. A week later she returned the pin mounted in a square of blue cloth, with a note saying she had been right about many things and expected a more informative acknowledgment if I wished to keep corresponding. I left the little parcel beside the blue ledger rather than in the drawer for worthless curiosities. Occasionally one meets a person with so little taste for deference that one is driven, against inclination, to be exact.

## 1870

### E03-006 | A Paper with No Title | 1870-01

Kappel sent an unsigned packet on the restoration of interrupted processes, the pages tied without his customary green string. Someone had written in the margin, 'Ask what restores the pattern.' I imagined he wanted a paper in which I would proclaim victory over his former masters. I drafted one under a false initial and asked him to circulate it without my name. His reply was brief: 'I did not ask you to lie.' An absurdly inconvenient distinction. He had preserved my access to certain records, yet apparently expected me to preserve an ethical boundary which the university itself had never scrupled to cross. I burnt the false title page and kept the essay. It remained unfinished because I could not decide whether anonymity was prudence or surrender.

### E22-003 | A Meal That Went Cold | 1870-01

Rosa carried a covered pot upstairs when the frost made the kitchen impossible. I was examining a little chamber whose pressure had scarcely altered in three hours. The cabbage smelled of cloves; its steam obscured the lower figures of my gauge. I directed her to leave it and remain silent. By the time I reached for the spoon, a gray skin had formed over the broth and a fly lay drowned at its edge. No meaningful change had occurred in the vessel. I ate standing, with the clock weight tapping the cupboard at every swing. Rosa's next delivery bore a scrap of paper: 'Prepared at eleven. Cold by one.' She continued this unnecessary chronological tyranny for years. I sometimes kept the slips in the blue ledger. They are not, of course, scientific records, though they have proved unusually accurate.

### E20-001 | A Better Failure in Winter | 1870-02

I had reconstructed a promising university trial with every dimension copied from the blue ledger. It failed before the first week ended. Lenz pointed to a fern of ice upon the northern window and asked which page of my ledger mentioned the temperature of a ruined tower. I had entered the measurements of the vessel, the weight of its fittings and the hour of observation, but not the simple fact that my old laboratory was warm. The stopper had contracted and cracked. I blamed him for the glass, although the stopper was of my choosing. We replaced the vessel and repeated the observation with a different result, no more satisfactory but at least intelligible. I preserved the split stopper in the drawer marked WINTER. A foolish name, but it spared me from calling every inconvenience persecution.

### E11-004 | The Chair at the Window | 1870-04

I set a second chair beside the east window for Kappel's visit, though the old man seldom stayed more than half an hour. He failed to come one afternoon and then another. I left the chair in its place because it held a stack of books rather well. On the third Thursday I found myself saying aloud that his remarks about the arrangement of my index were wrong. The Queen looked up from her ledge and resumed her occupations. There was no harm in speaking to one's specimens, provided one did not mistake a movement for comprehension. When Kappel finally arrived, suffering from a cough that made the stairs laborious, I began my argument before he had removed his gloves. He asked for tea. I made it badly and was rewarded with a criticism of the leaves. It was a satisfactory hour. The chair remained by the window long after his departure, a convenience which I continued to attribute to the books.

### E03-007 | Wartime Windows | 1870-08

Wagons took the road south through the summer, and every village conversation drifted toward the war with France. Lenz's brother had gone with a unit, and the glassworks had lost two trained hands. My latest vessel could not be delivered. I first treated this as an outrageous interference with science and then saw Lenz reading a letter that had passed through too many hands. We repaired what we possessed instead. I taught him my system for recording the service life of a vessel, and he taught me to mend a chipped rim without pretending it was whole. His brother survived that campaign, though we did not know it then. The delay prevented a risky transfer I had been impatient to attempt. I was not grateful to the war for this benefit.

### E20-002 | The Unbeautiful Survivor | 1870-09

The ugliest thing in the cabinet endured. It was neither human nor even promisingly animal, merely an organized tangle which grew against the glass like a careless signature. Its symmetrical companions had failed long before winter. Clara moved it to the south window and covered the other empty jars with linen. I told her it was an insult to my method. She answered that it appeared to be having an exceptionally good life without consulting my opinion. One evening I caught myself making a drawing of the twisted root with uncommon care, following each detour of its outline. I did not make it beautiful. I made it exact, which gave a pleasure I had not expected. I still would not have shown it to an admirer of my miniature Queen. I was beginning to learn how many audiences I kept inside my own head.

### E11-005 | The Supper Given Away | 1870-11

During a lean month I preserved enough money for lamp oil by economizing on supper. Clara discovered this when she found bread hardened beside the sink and my hands shaking while I tried to set a stopcock. She said I was unfit to handle delicate glass until I had eaten. I accused her of treating me like a patient; she replied that patients occasionally possessed the sense to follow instructions. Later that evening a farm boy brought a parcel of food from Lenz's household and refused a tip. I gave him my portion of cheese for the road, then regretted it before he reached the gate. I ate the bread and preserved the lamps. There was no nobility in the transaction. The following day my hands were steady and the experiment behaved. I began keeping provisions near the workroom, which I represented to visitors as a contribution to experimental reliability rather than obedience to Clara.

### E22-004 | The Room with No Witness | 1870-11

The first result was promising; the second, conducted without witnesses, more promising still. By the third trial I had written a sentence explaining what the next measurement ought to show. Lenz arrived to replace a spindle and I allowed him to remain. The needle trembled but did not rise. I struck the sentence from my notebook and accused the instrument of having been disturbed by his boots. He asked whether his boots had also disturbed the two earlier readings. It was a question designed by Providence to test my charitable temperament. We repeated the trial after supper with his boots beneath the table and my notebook left open to the original prediction. The result did not improve. He wrote 'unchanged' in the margin, slowly enough to be insulting. I permitted the word to remain. A record without an inconvenient witness is merely a diary of wishes.

### E26-001 | What the Lamp Retained | 1870-12

I knocked a lamp while bringing a covered vessel past the iron stand. The oil escaped beneath it, and its smell rose sharp and greasy before I saw the little flame. Clara, who had come about an unpaid bill, smothered the burning patch while I stood calculating the value of the vessel. Almost nothing was destroyed. I nevertheless insisted that the stand's uneven foot had caused the accident. Clara touched the level table with one finger. My hands were trembling, an observation I omitted from the accident ledger. That winter I moved the lamp away from the passage and called the change an improvement in working light. The smell of lamp oil later found me in rooms where nothing was burning. I cannot readily separate the danger I actually felt from the danger I now believe a thoughtful man should have recognized.

## 1871

### E03-008 | The New Empire on Cheap Paper | 1871-02

Clara brought news of the German Empire printed on paper so thin that daylight showed through the ink. She expected me to make an offensive remark and I obliged. Yet she had not come for an argument about emperors. The new postal arrangements might make some scientific correspondence easier; the price of coal had risen again. We calculated accounts while the Archbishop knocked his staff against the glass in the next room. She asked what I proposed to call my private kingdom. I said that I had no wish to rule. The Queen chose that moment to turn her back upon me, and Clara folded the newspaper without smiling. I entered the coal price in the blue ledger beneath a grander heading than it deserved.

### E20-003 | The Chamber Too Large | 1871-03

We spent five days constructing a vessel proportioned for a figure ten times the customary size. I had drawn it beautifully, and had consulted Lenz about every pressure and joint. We had neglected to consult the stairway. The chamber passed through the workshop door and lodged at the turn above the first landing, where it remained like a very expensive coffin. Lenz measured the clear width again while I proposed taking down a section of stone. He refused to work upon the wall without an engineer. In the end we disassembled the apparatus in the rain and carried it upward in pieces, where the rebuilt frame proved less true than before. I entered its cost under INFRASTRUCTURE, an ugly word for an ugly afternoon. I have since measured the route of every apparatus before admiring its dimensions.

### E11-006 | A Name Lost in the Lists | 1871-04

Reuter asked me to inspect a series of records from men returning wounded after the war. One young assistant had been known to him by a nickname, yet the hospital's transfer lists had entered three different spellings of his name. The man's injury was visible; his subsequent whereabouts were not. I argued with the clerk over the mistaken initials until he permitted us to copy the surviving records. We located the assistant at another infirmary two days later. Reuter called it good fortune. I called it a victory over defective classification. The man himself was more interested in finding his spectacles than in our triumph. Clara supplied a pair from a drawer of abandoned possessions. I saw how an error in a ledger could turn an identifiable person into an administrative absence. The blue ledger acquired a separate index for those who handled or cared for my creatures. I did not call it sentimental.

### E03-009 | The Queen's Quiet Week | 1871-06

For six days the Queen scarcely moved. I inspected her vessel with the mortifying caution of a man whose entire theory of life might be refuted by a creature the size of a nail. Clara sat nearby reading a catalogue aloud, a droning account of roots and leaves. The Queen approached the wall nearest her voice. I said it was coincidence. Clara agreed with suspicious readiness and continued reading. In the following weeks the Queen sometimes approached certain voices, sometimes turned away. I could have staged a demonstration for visitors and announced that she knew us. Instead I moved my chair closer and kept accounts of the failures as well. There are secrets which an impatient scientist destroys merely by announcing their discovery.

### E22-005 | The Number after the Name | 1871-06

The young clerk insisted upon a number before he would release my parcel. It contained a series of stopped vessels, none of which would have interested him had they been made of diamonds. I supplied my surname and former appointment. He looked at the shelf, not at me, and repeated the number. His cuffs were wet from the morning rain. I imagined, briefly, taking him through the university halls and demonstrating the weight of the name he was so cheerfully disregarding. Instead I read the number from the receipt. The parcel had survived its journey intact. Thereafter I numbered my correspondence more carefully, sometimes replacing names with marks intelligible only to their recipients. This was prudent, if perhaps a little theatrical. The clerk, who never discovered either distinction, remained perfectly civil.

### E16-010 | The New Offence | 1871-08

The new imperial criminal code arrived in the district wrapped in all the language of administrative progress. Paragraph 175 was short enough to fit upon a scrap of paper. I copied its wording and placed the copy beside an older Bavarian legal digest, then spent the afternoon demonstrating to Clara that an empire could take an act previously outside that particular criminal prohibition and manufacture an offender by printing a sentence. She replied that a gentleman with a tower could afford certain sorts of indignation more readily than a clerk with a family. At the post office a man was asked, within my hearing, why he received so many letters from the same address. Nothing came of the question. I remembered it nevertheless. That evening I sealed a letter to Theodor, intending only to tell him of the changed statute. I did not post it. I was angry at the law, and somewhat angrier that my first impulse had been to imagine my old companion in danger, rather than to ask whether he was content.

### E13-016 | A Voice Without a Stage | 1871-09

Leonie came to the tower after a dispute with the theatre manager, bringing a small box of stage pins and the curious exhaustion of a person who has been treated as indispensable and underpaid in the same hour. I displayed the Queen, who was not inclined to entertain anyone that day. Leonie watched her sit motionless in a corner, then announced that this was the most honest performance she had attended all season. Clara put soup upon the table. For once I did not lecture. Leonie spoke of a singer whose voice had failed, an apprentice who had married badly, and a room whose roof leaked upon the musicians. She asked whether I was happy in the tower. I said it was peaceful. She asked whether I was happy. I poured more soup and informed her that the question had not improved through repetition. She did not pursue it. After they left, I moved the Queen's jar from the cabinet to the table and sat where Leonie had sat. This was an alteration to my working arrangements, not a confession.

### E11-007 | The Queen's Evening | 1871-11

After the Queen's summer quiet spell, I began checking her platform before extinguishing the final lamp. At first I did so to determine whether her preferred position varied with the fall of light. One evening Clara watched me move the small screen, warm the outer glass with my palm, and place a scrap of bright cloth where the Queen had once selected it. 'You always do that in that order,' she said. I had not noticed. I replied that established methods deserve repetition. Clara asked whether I had tested another order. I said there was no reason to disturb a successful arrangement. The Queen settled near the cloth. I recorded nothing in the scientific column that night. What had begun as a control condition had become a ceremony whose interruption made me impatient. I retained it because the little creature appeared to prosper, not because I wished to be required at a particular hour.

### E22-006 | A Crown without an Audience | 1871-12

I spent an afternoon arranging velvet threads about the Queen's chamber, then fashioned a dais of admirable proportion. She examined it once, touched the edge, and retired to an unpainted corner where a torn strip of paper bent away from the glass. I had left the paper there as an expedient. Its ungainly edge apparently served some purpose of hers. Twice I removed it, and twice she became still. At last I restored it without improving the workmanship. Under snowlight the royal apartment looked faintly absurd, a magnificent throne beside a shabby shelter. I showed visitors the throne. When alone, I watched the shelter. The difference between what I displayed and what she inhabited was not, I decided, a defect of the Queen. I could not find a more satisfactory theory of taste.

## 1872

### E03-010 | The Festival I Did Not Attend | 1872-01

Lenz invited me to his family's winter gathering. I declined because the Mermaid's water was changing color and I could not bear to leave the tower. By evening the color had returned to normal. I could have gone, but I remained with my notebook and a plate of hard cheese. On Monday Clara brought the uneaten end of a cake and said Lenz had saved it for me. I informed her that attendance at festivities was not a professional obligation. She replied that nobody had suggested it was. The cake was surprisingly good. I sent Lenz a small repair instrument in return but never admitted that I had wanted to hear his family quarrel over cards.

### E14-001 | The Undertaker's Bad Latin | 1872-03

I met Georg Eberle because he had placed an advertisement offering obsolete glass for sale and I misread his occupation. He was sexton and caretaker of the old cemetery, not an instrument dealer. He showed me a row of cloudy vessels recovered from a chapel storeroom, then a register whose Latin headings had been copied incorrectly for twenty years. I corrected one. He thanked me and asked whether the correction made anyone less dead. I said it might make their survivors less misinformed, which seemed reason enough. Georg had one short leg and went down the cemetery steps sideways, neither apologizing for his gait nor requesting assistance. He offered the use of a dry undercroft for examining old stonework, provided I touched no grave and paid for my own candles. This was a remarkably sound contract. I bought the glass, though most was useless, and returned three weeks later with the proper Latin headings written on stiff card. He adopted them without admitting that this had pleased him. I understood the procedure.

### E11-008 | A Child with the Wrong Question | 1872-04

Lenz's little daughter was permitted to see a drawing of the King, not the actual glass vessel, since her father believed my laboratory unsuitable for children. She asked why the King was made so small. I described the advantages of observing life within controlled limits. She asked why he could not come out. I explained scale, temperature, and the dangers of a careless world. She considered this and said that a bird kept in a narrow cage might also be easy to observe. Her father told her not to be rude. I assured him I had been insulted more intelligently by older people. That evening I drafted a plan for a room-sized enclosure and found the expense intolerable. I put the drawing away. For some months thereafter I found myself staring at the glass rather than through it. It would be inaccurate to say the child's question changed my convictions. It did alter the location at which the discomfort appeared.

### E03-011 | An Inventory of Small Preferences | 1872-05

I entered my six little subjects in the blue ledger with their principal inclinations: the Queen's ledge, the King's devotion, the Archbishop's interruptions, the Devil's mirror, the Ballerina's music, and the Mermaid's preference for seclusion. It was an elegant chart. Clara ruined it by adding a column for exceptions. The Ballerina would not always dance in the cold; the Devil forgot his reflection when the King was active; the Mermaid appeared in bright light if nobody approached. I demanded that the exceptions be measured properly, and for three days no work of immediate ambition was done. The table became more useful and decidedly uglier. I retained it. A monarch, a devil, and a dancing girl had contrived to make a fool of the neatest account of their characters.

### E20-004 | The Queen's False Silence | 1872-06

A young physician declared that the Queen had grown dull. She remained motionless for his inspection and moved freely an hour after he had gone. I described her recovery to Clara, who asked whether the creature might be tired of us. Naturally I replied that fatigue had not been established. Still, I moved the clock, dismissed callers, and watched from behind a screen until the afternoon light changed. The Queen climbed her ledge, made an orderly circuit and returned to her customary corner. I had learned a valuable caution and felt very little satisfaction in it. A genius whose proof depends upon a subject appearing on command owns not a discovery but a small theatre with an unreliable performer. I did not entirely abandon the theatre.

### E06-001 | A Printer's Daughter | 1872-08

A bookseller sent me to the Rosen shop because its proprietor would accept an order that respectable printers declined. I expected an old conspirator and found his daughter Mathilde removing type from a forme with fingers black to the second joint. She had read the unsigned paper I had circulated and identified a numeral reproduced incorrectly in three places. Her father, she said, would print anything provided the customer paid, but she preferred a manuscript that knew the difference between seven and seventeen. I suggested that no intelligent reader could have mistaken my meaning. She set both numbers beside one another in metal type. Under the shop's hard northern light I saw how very little intelligence a reader required to ruin a result. I ordered thirty private copies and forbade her to improve my punctuation. The corrected numeral was hers, though the cover carried only my initials.

### E14-002 | Dinner Among the Stones | 1872-10

Rain detained me in Georg's undercroft while I waited for a mason to bring measurements of a sealed arch. The mason never arrived. I had bread, pickled onions, a tin of sardines, and a bottle which Georg identified as a respectable gin only after he tasted it. Leonie appeared looking for the caretaker to whom she had promised two repaired funeral hangings. She stopped at the sight of our supper spread across a broad stone lid and accused me of selecting my dining companions for their inability to contradict me. Georg, very much alive, contradicted her immediately. She sat on a folded sack and ate an onion. We discussed roof repairs, funeral fees, and the absurd custom of spending a dead man's remaining money upon a monument larger than his house. The damp made my fingers ache. I remember laughing at something Georg said, although not what it was. There was no ceremony and no triumph. On later solitary visits, a meal among tombs seemed entirely natural. A sensible location is not invalidated by its reputation.

### E22-007 | The Printer's Unfinished Line | 1872-10

Mathilde had set the last paragraph when I discovered that the sentence I most admired belonged to a result not yet obtained. I wished to retain it as a statement of reasonable expectation. She asked whether I should call that reasonable expectation a conclusion when it appeared below my signature. The shop smelled of wet paper and discarded orange peel. The compositor had already distributed half the type for another commission. I removed the sentence. Mathilde charged me for the second setting without apology, though she allowed me to keep the spoiled proof. Years later I found it in the tower with the magnificent last line crossed out so heavily that the page was almost torn. I remembered the cost more readily than the embarrassment. That is either evidence of thrift or one of the deficiencies of autobiographical recollection.

### E25-023 | Mathilde's Shorter Sentence | 1872-10

Mathilde Rosen was correcting a new pamphlet of mine when she asked why I had retained a clipping from Paris in the same packet as the proofs. I said it was evidence in a private professional dispute. She read the passage concerning the scientist's right to determine the purpose of his own inquiries, then held it against a sentence in my current essay. They were embarrassingly alike. 'You have been answering this man for five years,' she observed. I informed her that the resemblance was accidental. The type smelled of ink and the iron press made a steady noise below us. She removed one self-congratulatory adverb from the new sentence. I demanded it be restored, then let the correction stand after reading the line in proof. I had hired a printer and obtained a reader capable of detecting when my argument was an old quarrel disguised as new science. She never asked whether I had liked Jolivet. I should have resented such a question much less than I feared.

### E06-002 | The Price of a Name | 1872-11

On collecting the copies I found one sheet laid aside, a proof headed with my full name. Mathilde had set it to see whether my initials occupied less space. They did not. Kappel, who had accompanied me on the unlikely pretext of buying envelopes, asked whether I wished to hide the argument or its author. An offensive distinction, particularly when delivered before a printer's daughter. I told him that a signed statement would give the faculty another occasion to pronounce upon things they could neither measure nor mend. Mathilde asked why, in that event, I had placed my address beneath the final paragraph. I had meant to invite correspondence. I removed the address from twenty copies and preserved it on ten. I received one hostile letter and two requests for particulars, all sent to Kappel, who claimed this vindicated the experiment in honesty.

### E11-009 | An Invoice for Silence | 1872-12

Mathilde had completed a small private impression of my experimental notes. She offered to omit the shop's mark from the final copies if I paid for the additional work. I asked whether secrecy carried a tariff. She said so did paper, type, and the risk of explaining a mysterious order to a magistrate. Kappel, examining a proof, suggested that a few copies be given to credible witnesses rather than hidden in cupboards. I disliked the notion that I needed witnesses and ordered twelve extra copies. Mathilde kept one sealed in her shop, with a date and a list of corrections. I objected to her retaining my work. She replied that a printer who cannot prove what he printed is a convenient scapegoat. Years later I would say that no university deserved to control my name. When a date was disputed years later, Mathilde produced her sealed proof without troubling to look pleased.

## 1873

### E06-003 | The Old Automaton | 1873-01

Mathilde arrived carrying Hoffmann's tale of the Sandman and a mechanical bird whose owner had tired of winding it. She regarded the doll Olympia as a rather transparent indictment of men who prefer agreeable women to attentive ones. Lenz opened the bird and showed us a spring worn almost smooth where the mechanism failed to catch. I proposed that a genuinely living creature must repair some portion of its own disturbance. Mathilde pointed out that people also required repairs from others; she tapped Lenz's old burn scar without asking permission. We set the bird upon the window ledge, and its beak moved three times before falling silent. The King meanwhile knocked upon his partition until the Archbishop turned away. I disliked how easily the company transferred its attention from a machine to my little court, but I was not foolish enough to demand an explanation from a bird.

### E14-003 | The Doctor in the Funeral Book | 1873-02

Georg discovered that the dates recorded for a retired physician differed between a burial register and a charitable hospital's accounts. Reuter offered to check the hospital entry. I dismissed the discrepancy as a clerk's ordinary incompetence until the supposed physician's widow came to complain that a pension had ceased a month before his death. Dates, which are so tedious when accurate, become suddenly fascinating when someone earns money by altering them. Reuter found the hospital copy intact. Georg found the cemetery entry had been scraped and rewritten. I took impressions of the damaged paper, a labor requiring more patience than ingenuity. We could not identify who had made the alteration, but the widow obtained a hearing. Georg thanked Reuter and me with the same expression, which was almost an insult. I had come to study preservation of tissue, yet the decisive instrument that week was a dull pencil rubbed across a slip of paper. I later bought a better set of graphite sticks and pretended they were for laboratory diagrams.

### E03-012 | A Patient without a Name | 1873-03

Reuter invited me to review records of a patient who had repeatedly recovered after periods of apparently profound unresponsiveness. I went by a roundabout route, avoiding people who knew my dismissal. The patient was an elderly man who liked chess and disliked being spoken of as a case while present. Reuter introduced me as an old university colleague, neither defending nor condemning me. I asked the man whether he remembered the intervals. He remembered waking and finding the room changed, but nothing of what had happened between. I left with notes less triumphant than I had hoped. Reuter sent me away with a packet of clean linen for the tower. He had noticed that my cuffs were fraying. This small evidence of attention remained with me.

### E17-001 | The Borrowed Name | 1873-04

Mathilde introduced me to Max Reiss, a schoolteacher who wished to have a short defense of a dismissed colleague printed without identifying the author. Reiss was careful with his verbs and almost reckless with the number of copies he proposed to circulate. I asked whether the colleague had broken the law. He said that he did not know, and that the allegation had preceded any examination. Mathilde declined to print a name without the man's consent. I proposed a general argument about accusations unsupported by evidence and spent three nights correcting it. Reiss thought the piece too cold to influence the public. Mathilde said that my colder language might keep the teacher employed. We printed fewer copies than Reiss wanted. Weeks later he reported that the school had quietly ceased asking questions. I claimed no victory; no public correction had appeared. What I retained was Mathilde's insistence that the person concerned should choose how much of himself the pamphlet spent.

### E22-008 | The Station Porter's Complaint | 1873-04

A porter with a scar on his chin declined to take my crate. He did not know what lay inside, but a sharp medicinal odor rose from one corner and the straw was damp. I assured him the material was harmless. He asked whether I would be willing to lift it onto his cart without gloves. I changed the subject to his company's inefficiencies and persuaded no one. Rosa happened to be collecting flour sacks. She furnished clean straw and a dry cloth, then waited while I repacked the vessels myself. The transaction cost half a day and an amount of pride not entered in the ledger. Thereafter I required double wrappings and allowed 'FRAGILE GLASS' to appear on some consignments. The porter's scar has remained in my mind more clearly than his name. I believe he was quite right about the crate, which is no reason to admire his manner.

### E11-010 | A Patient's Complaint | 1873-05

The patient whose unusual periods of insensibility had interested me returned while I was visiting Reuter. He had become quite recovered and wished to know why a stranger had been shown particulars of his treatment. Reuter explained that I was a scientific consultant. The patient asked whether this entitled me to write about his body. I assured him the notes bore no name. He pointed to his occupation and age, which narrowed the possibilities in the town to perhaps three men. I had regarded anonymity as a mechanical matter of removing letters. Reuter asked me to correct the report before circulation, and I did, though I resented the time. Afterward I removed occupations from other case notes where they contributed nothing to the phenomenon. I remained convinced that useful knowledge ought not perish for want of access. I removed two unnecessary particulars from the next report. The pen nearly tore the page where I pressed too hard.

### E06-004 | A Door with Two Accounts | 1873-06

Clara lent her rear table for a meeting with Mathilde because the printshop's landlord had developed an appetite for listening through walls. Her dispensary ledger lay open at the edge of the table, each unpaid bottle entered against a name. Mathilde wanted to publish a clear description of the homunculi, including the circumstances under which they refused to move. Clara asked whether the report would expose them to visitors. I said a glass vessel could not be injured by the attention of a gentleman who understood glass. Both women looked at me until I amended the claim to the animals within it. We discussed paper dimensions instead. Clara measured her charges by what they could bear; Mathilde by what could be read; I by what could be proved. We reached no philosophy that afternoon. We agreed, rather less nobly, to use numbers instead of names in any public account.

### E20-005 | The Living Specimen and the Silk | 1873-07

Mathilde came with a proof of my account of miniature differentiation. I had described a series of splendid appearances, each one supported by precise adjectives and very little troublesome arithmetic. She tapped the passage in which I called two vessels successful, then placed beside it the record showing that both had ceased to respond. I asked whether a printer was now to adjudicate viability. She said a reader would do so if I gave him the opportunity. We worked through a rainy evening replacing my most handsome sentences with measurements and the names of failures. When I saw the result in type it had lost something of my elegance. It had gained the inconvenient property of being a document I could consult without remembering which claims I had embellished. We kept a copy beside the ledger.

### E14-004 | The Saint Who Refused the Bottle | 1873-08

Georg's cousin Matthias, a priest with uneven spectacles and a ruined umbrella, discovered me examining a damaged memorial near the gatehouse. He asked whether my work in the tower resembled the stories circulating about it. I offered him gin and the distinction between a story and evidence. He declined the gin, accepted the distinction, and asked to see an observation book. I produced one which contained no secrets of preparation, merely measurements. He spent half an hour asking questions of surprising pertinence. Then he said that if the little beings could suffer, my obligations to them might be greater, not less, than a parent's obligations to a child. I observed that parenthood was an analogy with considerable sentimental baggage. He replied that I had chosen to use the analogy of creation in the first place. I disliked the conversation. I invited him to return when I had less time to waste. He did not interpret this as refusal. His spectacles were repaired before his next visit. I was almost disappointed.

### E22-009 | An Hour for a Sick Visitor | 1873-09

Emil came up the path without his customary certainty and sat before I had offered a chair. He said the hill had been steeper than usual. When he tried to examine my tables, the page shook in his fingers. I accused him of having arrived ill merely to escape admitting an error, then brought water in a jug with a crack down its side. Clara found him an hour later, ordered the window opened, and treated my objections as an atmospheric nuisance. I gave Emil the guest bed and covered the specimen table in the adjoining room. Two days passed without the intended demonstration. On leaving, he thanked me for the bed but said nothing of our disagreement. I was offended by this selective memory. I have nevertheless kept a serviceable mattress in that room ever since.

### E03-013 | The Train Compartment | 1873-10

Lenz and I travelled by rail to collect an optical fitting. He insisted on facing forward and devoured sandwiches while I suffered the indignities of the carriage's motion. A little girl asked whether our case contained a bird; Lenz said it contained a thing considerably more troublesome. I was charmed until she demanded that we open it. At the workshop we discovered they had repaired the wrong fitting. I gave the proprietor an account of incompetence which ought to have been published for the benefit of craftsmen throughout Europe. Lenz produced the original order with its number and obtained a correction in four sentences. On the return journey he wrote that number inside the case. I permitted it. I did not permit him to describe the whole day as wasted, because we had acquired an excellent story at the proprietor's expense.

### E11-011 | The Parcel Not Opened | 1873-11

A parcel marked with Kappel's hand arrived damp and partly crushed. I assumed it contained another catalogue of obscure dissertations and left it under the stairs while I dealt with a failed preparation. Three days later Lenz used the box to prop open a door. Kappel arrived and asked whether I had read the letter within. It concerned an offer of privately held instruments whose owner would accept a visit only before the end of the month. We had missed the opportunity. I accused the rain, the messenger, and Lenz's mechanical ingenuity. Kappel asked why none of them had prevented my opening the parcel. I dislike questions which have only one answer and are therefore disguised accusations. I thereafter sorted letters by the sender's handwriting before attending to their scientific subject. Some senders mattered because of their knowledge. Others mattered because they would be offended, or absent, if neglected long enough.

### E06-005 | The Volume without a Spine | 1873-12

Kappel brought out a bound collection of withdrawn anatomical reports, its spine removed to avoid a title on the shelf. Between two accounts of failed grafts lay a memorandum I remembered arguing against as a student. I now found its descriptions of interrupted recovery remarkably sound. Mathilde, who had come to inspect the paper stock, asked why I had underlined the author's error but not his correct prediction. I told her that mistakes invite marks more urgently than commonplaces. She put a small cross beside the neglected passage. Later, alone, I compared it with three records from the Queen's early months. The resemblance was insufficient for a conclusion and sufficiently irritating to occupy my Christmas holiday. I copied the memorandum, leaving its author's name intact. Kappel noticed. He said nothing, which was the most agreeable compliment I ever received from him.

### E26-002 | A Supper Measured in Coal | 1873-12

The kitchen had been cold for two days because I preferred to spend the coal money on a polished glass fitting. Rosa found me eating a crust beside broth which had formed a skin. She opened the stove and announced that the ashes were nearly white. The smell of wet coal came in with her boots. I said the fitting might advance our work by months. She asked whether it would warm a room in December. My stomach answered before I could. Rosa put half a measure on the fire and entered the debt in her own book. We ate bread while the pipes ticked and the room gradually became inhabitable. I repaid her after Christmas. Thereafter I began ordering fuel before ornamental brass. I described this as economy. Rosa said I was finally acquiring the rudiments of winter.

### E14-005 | The Devil at Vespers | 1874-01

Matthias asked me to look at a heating pipe in his church because Georg thought the metal had been installed improperly. I attended after vespers, when the last parishioners were collecting their shawls. A painted devil above the doorway had been restored so clumsily that he appeared to be smiling at the congregation. We examined the pipe and found a leak where condensation had softened the plaster. Georg fetched a bucket. I was obliged to stand upon the church's ladder while the priest steadied its feet. From above I could see the painted saint and devil together, each accusing the other of bad company. ### E03-014 | The Archbishop Changes Sides | 1874-02

The Archbishop, long the King's adversary, surprised me by standing between the King and a falling piece of miniature furniture. I first thought the gesture accidental. Then he helped push the object against the wall. The King spent the evening beside him rather than beside the Queen. I watched this unlikely truce until the lamp oil failed. Clara urged me not to describe a single cooperative action as reconciliation. I obeyed in writing and disobeyed in conversation. By the end of the month the two had returned to their usual disputes, but sometimes shared a ledge without quarrelling. I made a new observation card instead of erasing the old one. Their history had developed complications inconvenient to a simple allegory.

### E22-010 | The Bishop's Supper | 1874-02

The Archbishop had long seemed to regard the King's courtship as a public scandal. One winter evening, however, he remained still while the King approached the Queen, provided the meal had been moved away from the bright side of the vessel. I first interpreted this as an ecclesiastical conversion. It was an agreeable theory and quite without evidence. The bell of a distant chapel struck the hour, and the Archbishop turned toward the darker glass rather than toward either sovereign. I moved the bread again the following week and observed the same result. Whether he objected to romance, glare, or bad dining arrangements, I could not say. I continued describing him as disapproving to visitors; he was a better character that way. My private notes acquired a fourth column marked 'conditions of objection.' It was less entertaining and considerably more useful.

### E06-006 | The Archbishop's Audience | 1874-04

Mathilde asked to see the beings about which she had been setting type for eighteen months. I permitted the visit at noon, when the light was steady, and arranged the Archbishop's chamber nearest the table. The little ecclesiastic stood motionless until the King stumbled against a narrow ledge. He then crossed the enclosure and laid one hand upon the rail, an act that looked wonderfully like assistance and might equally have been possession of a favored object. Mathilde whispered, with exasperating delight, that my bishop had better instincts than some professors. I wished to correct her, but the King was already upright. I wrote the precise order of movement, and Lenz quietly widened the dangerous ledge while my guest was examining the Queen. She left without demanding proof of souls. She did request that I remove the word 'obedient' from the printer's proof.

### E11-012 | The Archbishop's Place | 1874-06

Mathilde returned to observe the little Archbishop, who had recently surprised us by protecting rather than obstructing the King. She wanted a clean account of the change for the next impression of my notes. I offered a theory of rank and allegiance. Clara remarked that the Archbishop had stood where the strongest current of warm air entered the vessel, and that his position may have had nothing to do with the King. We arranged another observation and found that both explanations fit some of the movements. Mathilde set the paragraph with the words 'cannot presently distinguish.' ### E06-007 | A Review Written in Advance | 1874-07

An editor from Augsburg returned our prospectus with the assurance that claims of miniature men could be dismissed without an inspection. He had, however, complimented my literary manner, which showed him capable of good judgment in a narrow field. Mathilde had already composed a rebuttal before I arrived. She proposed to print the rejection beside an inventory of witnessed behaviors. I advised her that humiliation was more effective when administered sparingly; she replied that I had furnished enough for a lifetime. We quarrelled over whether the editor's name belonged on the sheet. She wished to print it. I did not wish to give a mediocrity the immortality of appearing in my pamphlet. ### E03-015 | The Question of a Larger Vessel | 1874-09

I commissioned a much larger vessel, believing the restrictions of scale might be partly architectural. Lenz warned that its walls would distort observation and its weight would make relocation hazardous. I persisted. When the glass arrived I could scarcely move it through the tower door. For two afternoons we manoeuvred it with padded boards before Clara asked whether I had calculated how we would remove it in an emergency. I had not. ### E20-006 | The Meal in the Crypt | 1874-09

Georg found me eating cold chicken beneath the stone ribs of the crypt, with a cigar balanced in the little hollow between two names. He objected to the ash, not to the meal. I moved the cigar and offered him the less respectable half of the bird. He told me about the cemetery wall, which had begun to settle, and the widow who insisted her husband's grave should face her kitchen window. Neither subject involved the soul, death's boundary, or my achievements. I found the conversation restorative. When he left I examined a stain of grease on the cuff of my coat and decided that conventional dining rooms were equally capable of indignity at greater cost. ### E14-006 | A Misplaced Blessing | 1874-11

Matthias came to observe the Archbishop, whom I expected to regard with a theological anxiety appropriate to his profession. The little ecclesiastic had instead turned his back upon the King and arranged himself beside a plain stone. Matthias watched silently for a quarter of an hour, then asked what permitted me to call the creature an archbishop. ### E06-008 | The Bird That Would Not Sing | 1874-12

The mechanical bird ceased its thin whistle on a December afternoon. Mathilde blamed the coal dust. Lenz declared the spring sound and the bellows leaking. I knew that the sound had always been disagreeable and made no objection when the creature was taken apart upon the shop counter. The fault proved to be a fragment of thread wound about the axle. Mathilde removed it with a bodkin and gave the bird back its voice. She asked whether my Queen would recover as readily from an obstruction of equal scale. ### E22-011 | The Chicken and the Ledger | 1874-12

I carried a roast chicken wrapped in paper to the crypt because the rain had driven me from the hill, and no one there required conversation. Georg Eberle discovered me at the stone ledge with a wing in my hand and the blue ledger open between two old inscriptions. He did not demand to know why I preferred this company. He pointed out that grease had seeped through the paper onto a relative's name. I apologized to the stone rather than to Georg, an economy of civility he did not appreciate, and scrubbed the mark with water from a bucket. I kept the resulting stain on the ledger page. I have dined in respectable houses where the dead were discussed with greater appetite and less decency. Georg later put a small folding stool beside the crypt door. He denied that it was for me.

## 1875

### E03-016 | The Cold Soup Agreement | 1875-01

Clara discovered that I had not eaten properly for two days. She set soup on my desk and announced that she would stop bringing supplies if I continued treating food as an interruption. I objected that a man might choose his own habits. She agreed and said she might choose hers. We made an arrangement: I would eat before the second evening lamp was lit, and she would not rearrange my experiments without asking. The bargain lasted eleven days before I broke it. ### E11-013 | The Door after Supper | 1875-02

Clara had negotiated our arrangement about regular meals in January. I resented the suggestion that I required management and agreed to a fixed supper chiefly to end the discussion. A month later she failed to arrive at the appointed hour. I cooked an egg, burnt it, and decided not to consider the matter further. When she appeared late, having attended a difficult birth in the village, I greeted her with an unnecessarily elaborate complaint about punctuality. She asked whether I had been worried. I said I had merely wished to prevent waste. She ate the better part of the bread, then fell asleep in the chair for ten minutes. I covered the lamp because the light troubled her. On waking she noticed and smiled, which was most irritating. I have never liked to be caught providing comfort; it encourages the recipient to expect a repetition. Of course I covered the lamp again when the occasion arose.

### E20-007 | The King After the Fall | 1875-03

The King had long since survived his fall, yet one stiff movement continued to offend me. I devised a support finer than a spider's bridge and placed it where his gait might be corrected. Reuter watched the little fellow avoid it twice, then saw a fresh mark where the support had caught his limb. He told me to remove it. I demanded a reason better than medical timidity. He showed me the mark and waited. I removed the support. Over the next weeks the King moved more freely, though never with precisely the grace I had intended. I considered this a temporary compromise. Reuter considered it evidence that the creature possessed a body and not merely a design. We were both correct. That did not make his silence during the procedure any less insulting.

### E06-009 | The Visitor from the River | 1875-04

A young boatman's wife came to Clara complaining that her husband had stopped answering during the long river journeys. Clara asked ordinary questions about sleep and food while I considered nervous exhaustion and the possibility of a more interesting lesion. The wife said that he answered everyone except her. It was a domestic insult rather than a medical novelty. Clara sent them home with instructions about rest and a bill smaller than it might have been. Mathilde, seated near the doorway, remarked that silence could be a choice, a symptom, or an accusation, and that a ledger should not confuse them. I had recently called the King's stillness deliberate refusal. Returning to the tower, I changed the entry to 'no movement observed.' I did not announce a reformation of my principles. The revised phrase was plain enough, and therefore sufficiently humiliating.

### E14-007 | The Borrowed Cigar Case | 1875-05

Georg produced a cigar case left by a visiting merchant and asked whether tobacco could be kept in a dry cupboard until its owner returned. I examined the workmanship before noticing that the smell had already settled into the leather. He invited me to try one. I informed him that cigars were the single weakness I permitted myself. Leonie, who had heard a similar announcement concerning gin, said my self-restraint was becoming statistically implausible. I replied that an exception need not remain the same exception throughout a lifetime. The first cigar was badly rolled, and the second, smoked a fortnight later, made me cough in a manner I considered private until Georg began imitating it. I subsequently purchased a better case and kept it beside the blue ledger. I cannot explain why the ornament of a vice should be more useful than the vice itself. Perhaps the case, unlike tobacco, could be opened before a conversation one did not wish to have. Several visitors learned to wait while I chose a cigar. Not all understood that I was choosing whether to answer them.

### E22-012 | The Kingdom's Winter Account | 1875-05

I had drawn up a schedule by which the tower could subsist on reduced coal, fewer replacement seals, and a diet of nearly heroic simplicity. Rosa examined it and asked whether the jars would be consuming the same coal as the gentleman who had drawn the columns. Lenz found three joints in the heating pipes approaching failure. I accused them both of concerted extortion. They replied with estimates, delivered separately and therefore doubly irritating. I sold the rights to a harmless old lecture and spent the money on repairs. During the first hard frost the heating continued without interruption. I maintained that my economy had succeeded, since fewer vessels were broken. Rosa informed me that this was a novel definition of economy. She brought sweet tea without charging for it. I never discovered whether that omission was kindness or an error.

### E03-017 | The Summer with No Answer | 1875-07

I wrote Henry a new letter about the limits of bodily scale. It took three drafts to remove a paragraph asking whether he had forgotten me. Kappel carried it into town because I had begun to dislike being seen at the post office. No answer came that summer. I interpreted the silence as contempt, then as busyness, then as deliberate caution. None of these interpretations could be tested. I filed a fourth draft, unsent, which contained only a question about his present work and a description of the Mermaid's habits. When autumn arrived I burned the draft but kept the envelope. I was proud of not pursuing him. Pride is a convenient name for a decision one cannot entirely defend.

### E17-002 | The Gentleman Who Was Not My Type | 1875-07

Reiss introduced me to a retired merchant at an outdoor table. The gentleman's chief intellectual accomplishment was the possession of a watch which chimed a quarter-hour inaccurately. He was also plainly interested in my company, an interest communicated through attentions too numerous to mistake. I attempted an elaborate description of my microscopic work to discourage him. Reiss watched the performance with visible amusement. On the way back he said I could simply have declined an invitation. I answered that plain refusal was frequently mistaken for an insult. Reiss said that my alternative had been an insult with diagrams. I considered whether the man's attention had displeased me because it was unwanted or because I disliked being seen to receive it. These were different questions. I did not settle either. At our next meeting I spoke to the merchant courteously, and he discovered my research tedious. We parted on terms that would have been difficult to improve through further analysis.

### E06-010 | The Last Line on the Page | 1875-09

Kappel had begun preserving my unsent letters to Henry between folders of neglected dissertations. I found the arrangement impertinent. A letter not sent, I observed, was not correspondence. It was draft material, and draft material belonged to its author. Kappel answered that my concern for ownership had never prevented me from requesting access to other men's discarded thoughts. Mathilde was present to collect the paper inventory. She counted four letters bearing Henry's name, each one shorter than its predecessor until the last consisted of a single unfinished question. I took all four home. Two weeks later I returned the first three to Kappel's archive, keeping the last in the blue ledger. I had no wish to place its question in another man's custody, even one who had long since earned the privilege of seeing worse things in me.

## 1876

### E06-011 | The Little Examination | 1876-02

Mathilde brought six blank cards, each bearing a different shape, and requested permission to hold them near the Queen's chamber in an order unknown to me. I consented because a result that arrives only when its author directs the sequence is a party trick, not knowledge. The Queen approached a dark triangle twice, avoided it once, and ignored the rest. The pattern did not survive the afternoon. Mathilde was disappointed. I was furious, but chiefly with the cards, which had been cut from inferior stock and bent in the damp. Lenz had prepared a second batch. We repeated the sequence on another day with no improvement. I recorded the failure and refused Mathilde's suggestion that we name the Queen's favorite shape. If I must bestow titles upon insignificant things, there are members of the faculty still available.

### E11-014 | The Hand That Would Not Settle | 1876-03

Lenz's hands trembled slightly after a long day at the furnace, and I noticed it while he attempted a delicate repair. I asked whether he had been drinking. He showed me the blister beneath his thumb and suggested that I try holding the hot rod myself before diagnosing his habits. Clara bound the hand. We postponed the work. At the next visit I brought a padded grip devised from cork and cloth. It was a poor contrivance but useful enough that he kept it. He thanked me with a joke about my becoming an apprentice at last. I replied that necessity had forced the descent. I did not inquire whether the grip lessened his pain; I watched his fingers while he worked and could see that it did. In my notebooks I recorded the alteration to the instrument. I did not record the reason I had designed it.

### E22-013 | A Disagreement over the Keys | 1876-03

I returned from an errand to find Clara in the upper room and the floor dry where a broken tap might have ruined a month's work. She had entered with Lenz's key, which I considered a trespass until she showed me the warped stopcock. I asked why she had not waited. She asked which of the drowned creatures she should have consulted for permission. Her coat smelled of wet wool and lamp soot. We spent the next hour drafting conditions under which she might enter in my absence, and I insisted on two signatures and an exact account of the keys. She signed without reading my most magnificent clauses. A second key hung afterward in her dispensary. I complained for weeks about the breach of scientific sovereignty. I also slept more easily on nights when the pipes knocked.

### E03-018 | The Repairman's Hand | 1876-04

Lenz injured two fingers on hot glass and for a fortnight could not make the joints upon which my experiments depended. I arrived at his shop armed with instructions about splints. His wife put a stack of unwrapped orders in front of me and said I might assist by keeping my medical imagination to myself. I wrapped glass, mislaid a label, and discovered that paper twine can humiliate a philosopher as thoroughly as a hostile faculty. I returned the next week and did the work with fewer mistakes. When Lenz offered to reduce my bill, I refused. I am not fond of debts whose amount cannot be entered in a ledger; moreover, I had no wish to teach him that injury reduced the value of his skill. His wife later sent better twine to my tower, with no explanation.

### E14-008 | The Address on the Funeral Notice | 1876-04

Mathilde Rosen brought a proof of a funeral notice to Georg because a printer's compositor had omitted the street number. I corrected the punctuation before she had finished explaining why the omission mattered. Mathilde told me that the family had already lost one relative who had been unable to find the new house; a misplaced mourner was not a metaphor but a person. Georg took the notice and checked it against the parish records. I asked Mathilde why she tolerated work so dull. She said that the dead rarely complained and the living complained quite enough for both parties. We walked back to the printing shop through puddles containing fragments of election posters. I bought a packet of envelopes I did not require, then asked her to send future proofs of my scientific pamphlets directly to the tower. She asked whether that meant I trusted her corrections. I said it meant I distrusted everybody else's. She regarded this as an adequate contractual answer, if not an especially gracious one.

### E06-012 | The Name in the Catalogue | 1876-06

A catalogue listed my private pamphlet under 'Pretorian curiosities,' a designation at once illiterate and insulting. The shop's apprentice had entered the heading from a secondhand dealer's note. Mathilde offered to correct it. I told her not to trouble herself, since a collector stupid enough to purchase curiosities would at least pay an honest price for the book. That evening I wrote the dealer an impeccably polite letter containing four separate demonstrations of his incompetence. Kappel later saw it and observed that I had made myself memorable to a man who might otherwise have forgotten me. He was quite right. Weeks afterward the corrected catalogue arrived with the category 'experimental natural philosophy.' I placed it between the uncorrected proof and my letter, a triptych considerably more satisfying than a public apology.

### E25-024 | The Catalogue's Wrong Title | 1876-06

A bookseller sent a catalogue in which my private work had been entered under a ridiculous heading among curiosities. I had an excellent mind to write an objection in twelve pages. While searching for the original publication date I found the 1867 offprint, still carrying the printer's fault in its heading, and the newspaper which had made me visible to readers who did not care about the difference between a spinal reflex and a marvel. I considered sending the bookseller Jolivet's feature as evidence that I had once enjoyed continental notice. What prevented me was the conclusion: the young professor was talented but governed poorly by his own will. Instead I enclosed the German paper alone. The bookseller amended the listing without apology. For the remainder of the afternoon I was disagreeably pleased that a name could be corrected by reference to a document rather than by performance of injured dignity. Then I performed it anyway to Clara over supper. She asked whether the catalogue had been fixed. I admitted it had.

### E06-013 | Ink after the Burn | 1876-09

Lenz's injured hand recovered well enough for work, though he could no longer close the little finger without discomfort. Mathilde brought him a ledger ruling device as a gift, only to discover that he preferred drawing lines with a straightedge held in the other hand. He showed her the small changes he had made to his bench. I remarked that adaptation of an instrument was preferable to romantic mourning over its limitations. Lenz asked whether I regarded the human hand as an instrument. I said I regarded it as an expensive apparatus insufficiently appreciated by its owner. He laughed, then used his uninjured hand to produce a new chamber stop precisely fitted to my request. I retained the earlier flawed stop as well. It reminded me that a workshop can change more reliably than a university, although I did not flatter either craftsman with the comparison.

### E26-003 | The Left Thumb | 1876-10

The new clamp behaved perfectly until its fifth adjustment. Then the brass grew hot enough to mark my left thumb through thin leather. I dropped the screw and announced that the instrument had developed a flaw in its character. Lenz had warned me about the exposed joint. Clara brought cool water and declined to hear how minor the injury was. The pain pulsed with my heartbeat; holding a pen steady was suddenly difficult. For three days I had Lenz take the measurements and found myself correcting his handwriting instead of his results. The clamp acquired a guard. Years afterward a warm brass handle revived my irritation more efficiently than a letter from an enemy. I continued removing my protective glove whenever a visitor seemed likely to appreciate a demonstration of dexterity.

### E03-019 | The Ledger's Missing Page | 1876-11

I found one page missing from the blue ledger, the page on which I had entered the Queen's first sustained orientation. My immediate suspicion fell upon a visiting instrument seller. Clara pointed out that the binding had worn thin from years of use. Kappel, summoned like a magistrate to a crime scene, found the leaf pressed inside a botanical catalogue where I had used it as a marker. The relief was brief; the embarrassment, more durable. We began copying observations into a second register kept in town. I resented the duplication until a water leak ruined several loose papers that winter. The copy preserved the records. The leaf itself, still bearing the crease of its escape, remained in the first book.

### E20-008 | The Blind Naming | 1876-11

I claimed I could distinguish the promising vessels at a glance. Clara said this was the kind of claim made by men who never allowed anyone to rearrange a shelf. Lenz tied blue ribbons to the lids, marked the bottoms, and asked me to select the soundest specimens while the shutters stood half closed. I chose the most attractive three. Two had already failed. The plain vessel which I passed over continued its obscure growth for another month. I accused Lenz of allowing the light to mislead me. She opened the shutters and invited me to repeat the trial. I did better, though not well enough to justify the original boast. Lenz wrote the scores and showed no particular delight in defeating me. I found her lack of triumph peculiarly difficult to resent.

### E14-009 | The Queen at the Locked Door | 1876-12

The Queen had begun approaching the narrow service door whenever voices sounded beyond it. I proposed that she sought a particular frequency in Clara's speech. Matthias, present by invitation I had almost regretted issuing, wondered whether she simply expected company. I said the distinction was not testable in its present form. Clara offered to conduct a silent entrance. The Queen approached the door before Clara was visible and withdrew when the hinge stuck. We repeated the trial, and the results were neither beautiful nor conclusive. Matthias asked whether I had ever opened the door merely to see where she wished to go. I reminded him that the room beyond contained instruments, chemical baths, and hazards she could not understand. He conceded the danger, then asked who had decided that confinement was the only safe arrangement. I disliked the implication and wrote down the question. The next morning I placed a small empty passage between two enclosed platforms. The Queen inspected it for ten minutes, then returned to her original place. A refusal to travel is not an endorsement of the prison.

## 1877

### E06-014 | The Complaint behind Glass | 1877-01

One winter morning the King struck the glass with unusual regularity, stopping whenever the Archbishop approached. I suspected an exchange of signals and said as much. Clara thought the chamber too dry. Mathilde asked whether either hypothesis could explain why the behavior began only after the new stopper had been fitted. I examined the joint and discovered a narrow draft where the glass met the wax. There was nothing heroic in repairing it. The King ceased his tapping that evening; the Archbishop returned to his customary occupation of disapproval. Clara asked whether I would amend the record. I entered her suggested cause beside mine, with the date and no preferred conclusion. I then spent a quarter hour watching the King sit perfectly still, an ingrate with no concern whatever for the labor he had occasioned.

### E14-010 | A Sermon Without My Name | 1877-03

Matthias invited me to a lecture on charity delivered by a visiting prelate. I attended because I was assured no collection would be taken, which proved untrue. The speaker denounced people who pursued knowledge without regard for suffering. He had never heard of me, yet several listeners turned their heads in my direction, which suggested the local imagination required little prompting. I rose to ask how much suffering the institution had prevented by refusing inquiry into its causes. The prelate replied with an account of his hospital work, naming patients and expenses in embarrassing detail. I was prepared for piety, not figures. Afterward Georg said I had asked a fair question and received a fair answer. Matthias said an opponent need not be imaginary merely because one preferred him that way. I contributed money to the hospital fund and gave my name as a misspelling of Pretorius so no one would infer a conversion. The receipt arrived under the wrong spelling and I kept it. I cannot say why.

### E17-003 | The Letter with the Familiar Hand | 1877-03

A letter from Theodor arrived after years in which our correspondence had dwindled to notes at Christmas. He had secured a position supervising architectural drawings and was living with a man whose patience, he said, exceeded that of most professors. The sentence was not an accusation, though I spent a whole afternoon searching it for one. He enclosed a photograph of a building whose windows he had designed. It was handsome and insufficiently ventilated. I prepared a reply listing the defect, then put the sheet away. Clara, bringing fresh cloth for the jars, found me looking at the picture. I told her that the work was competently done. She asked whether I had said as much to him. I wrote him three sentences of praise and an additional paragraph about the windows. He answered in kind, correcting a detail I had misunderstood about the plan. We had lost the ease of our earlier life. We had not lost the pleasure of being exact together.

### E06-015 | A Newspaper Death | 1877-05

An obituary reported the death of a minor scholar who had once signed the petition for my removal. I recognized the signature with precision I could not summon for the man's face. Mathilde asked whether I should send his widow a letter. I answered that condolences from the victim of her husband's cowardice might be mistaken for triumph. Kappel said that perhaps I was afraid of precisely the opposite. We let the paper lie on the table while the press rattled. Later, passing the house where the dead man had lodged as a student, I discovered that I remembered him lending me an umbrella during a storm. I wrote no letter. I did, however, retrieve the damp obituary from the shop's rubbish and file it beside the petition. A death had not altered the signature; it had made the attached hand less conveniently abstract.

### E06-016 | A Volume Too Small to Burn | 1877-07

Mathilde bound the private record into a narrow volume in brown cloth. It contained no formulae, only dates, corrections, the names of those who had looked, and the things my small people had done when watched and when left alone. She called it a book of witnesses. I had intended a vindication. The distinction displeased me, though the paper was excellent. Clara ordered a copy for her own shelf, promising neither praise nor concealment. Kappel asked that a third be kept beyond Ingolstadt. I refused until Mathilde pointed out that I had already lost a page and nearly lost several years of correspondence. We placed a sealed copy with a dealer on the road west. At the end of the afternoon the mechanical bird, still unreliably musical, whistled once from the high shelf. It seemed an absurd fanfare, which did not prevent me from enjoying it.

### E03-020 | The Door Left Open | 1877-08

Lenz repaired the upper landing and, for the first time in years, we could carry a chair to the east window without fearing the floor. Clara opened the shutters. The road below was full of dust and the smell of cut grass, not the host of persecutors I had sometimes imagined. She asked whether I intended to spend the remainder of my life watching the world from a tower. I told her that I had plenty of work. She did not disagree. That evening I prepared a small travelling case with space for one vessel, a notebook and the metronome. I did not yet choose a destination. The case stood unopened beneath the window for months, an intention too delicate to be dignified with the name of a plan.

### E22-014 | The Unpublished Defeat | 1877-08

Mathilde appeared with a copied paragraph in her careful hand. I had written to a correspondent that a specimen's movement survived without a certain light source. My own ledger suggested that it had survived only when I failed to measure the light properly. I wished to annotate the ledger and consider the problem at leisure. Mathilde had already addressed an envelope. She informed me that the recipient possessed an incorrect claim with my name beneath it. I sent the correction, but refused her proposal for a printed notice that would have satisfied the most vindictive conscience. We argued over the sealing wax until the glass inkstand tipped and stained the tablecloth. The stain remains a livid blue. I recall it whenever someone praises the unqualified honesty of scientific men, an opinion ordinarily advanced by men who have not published results.

### E14-011 | The Paper Skeleton | 1877-10

Leonie brought Georg a paper skeleton intended for an educational exhibition. The figure had brass fasteners at the joints and an absurdly cheerful grin painted upon the skull. A teacher had refused it because children found the movable jaw entertaining. I asked why entertainment made the anatomy less accurate. Leonie said the teacher feared that the pupils would remember laughter instead of instruction. I moved the arms and demonstrated that the shoulder had been cut incorrectly. Georg suggested we repair it before insulting the teacher. We spent the afternoon adjusting joints with needles and bits of card. When the model finally raised both arms without tearing, Leonie bowed to it as though acknowledging a new stage performer. I laughed, then arranged the skull upon a chair to inspect the shadow cast by the lamp. For a moment it appeared to be considering an answer to a question no one had asked. I purchased the figure from Leonie. Later visitors assumed it was a morbid ornament. I did not trouble to correct them unless they touched the joints.

### E11-015 | The Invitation I Kept | 1877-11

After the late summer when I had finally allowed the tower door to remain open to visitors, invitations became a nuisance of surprising frequency. Lenz asked me to a family supper; Clara enclosed a note that no specimens would be discussed at the table. I wrote a refusal based on a perfectly real series of observations. Before sending it I found an old invitation from five years earlier among the bills, its corners worn by repeated handling. The experiment was not time-sensitive. I attended the supper with a bottle of gin I considered a respectable offering and discovered that Lenz's daughter had become quite opinionated about my manners. She asked why I always arrived wearing a coat suitable for an execution. I told her it improved the appearance of a happy gathering by contrast. She laughed. On returning to the tower I filed the invitation with correspondence rather than accounts. I laid the invitation among Kappel's letters, though it contained no argument or observation at all.

## 1878

### E04-001 | The Case Opened at Last | 1878-03

The travelling case had sat beneath the east window for months, becoming such a fixture that Clara threatened to fill it with winter linen. I took the train to Munich and examined a collection of preserved plants, having entrusted the lamps to Lenz and the creatures' arrangements to Clara. I removed the old cracked metronome at the station when its weight offended me; a cherished error need not travel first class. Most of the collection was disappointing. One conservator, however, showed me how to record changes in illumination without disturbing a specimen. I copied his method and improved his chart on the journey home. The Queen had survived my absence without apparent distress. I called this proof that my arrangements were excellent. It was also a somewhat chilly welcome.

### E20-009 | The Garden of Discarded Shapes | 1878-05

Clara entered the neglected glass room and found three cultures I had labelled worthless. Their intended shapes had come to nothing. Yet the moss beside one vessel had changed its color, and another had preserved moisture in a place that ordinarily dried by noon. We could not tell which effect belonged to the growth itself and which to my careless storage. I instructed her to discard the whole collection. She asked whether I proposed destroying the observations as well. I kept five vessels and called the result an ecological trial, a term sufficiently grand to conceal that I had been wrong about rubbish. They occupied the west shelf for years. No little man emerged from them, and it would be false to claim they were our finest specimens. They taught me more about the room than any mirror-polished head ever had.

### E22-015 | The Case of Small Necessities | 1878-07

Marta Voss arrived with a botanical press, worn gloves, and questions so unromantic that I almost sent her away. She wished to know how often the water was changed, what the room smelled like before dawn, and whether the occupants behaved differently after a week of quiet. I had prepared an explanation of their extraordinary origins. She interrupted it to ask whether a damaged latch frightened them. We inspected the lower chambers first. Dried thyme from her satchel scented the room, masking the familiar sourness of the jars, and I had to admit that I had ceased noticing the latter. I gave her the maintenance book, not the secret notebook. The distinction seemed elegant. She returned it covered with marginal inquiries, every one about the stubborn business of keeping something alive. I answered them all, though not in the order she preferred.

### E04-002 | The Botanist with Ink on Her Sleeve | 1878-09

Marta Voss had catalogued a botanical collection dismissed as too difficult for a woman by three gentlemen who could not identify half its specimens. I met her over a disputed entry. She showed me a dormant preparation recovered after a longer interval than I would have predicted, then produced the measurements before I could accuse her of relying on anecdote. Better still, she had read an unsigned paper of mine and found a fault in its conclusion without insulting its observations. At luncheon I asked for her address. She required, as a condition of correspondence, that I cease using the word 'provincial' to mean 'contrary to Pretorius.' I accepted no such constitutional amendment, but posted her a packet within the fortnight. She answered it, and in ink dark enough to be a challenge.

### E14-012 | A Small Funeral for a Name | 1878-11

A man named Bernhard Vogel was buried on a morning so windy the priest's pages would not remain in order. I had never met him. He had copied some of my observations for a private catalogue and once sent a correction with the modest request that his name not be printed. Marta Voss remarked that he might have wished to protect his employment. Georg knew the family and said Vogel had disliked crowds in life. I went because his correction had saved me from an error in a botanical comparison, and because I thought attendance might be the only acknowledgement left available. There were eleven mourners, counting me. No one recognized my connection to the deceased, which initially offended me and then seemed appropriate. After the service I placed the corrected page inside my coat rather than leaving it on the grave. Voss asked what I had said to his widow. Nothing, I replied. She had enough people telling her what sort of man her husband had been. I thought it sufficient to have come. I could not persuade Voss that I meant this modestly.

### E22-016 | The Inventory Left Unfinished | 1878-12

Rosa spread flour sacks and invoices across the table while Voss examined the newest catalogue. Between them they had turned my pantry into an audit. I could account for every pane of glass, and nearly every spoonful of spirit used in preserving instruments. Voss asked where I had recorded the Queen's preference for the shabby paper corner. I said that preferences were not provisions. Rosa asked why I had purchased three yards of velvet if they were not. I began a separate page headed 'Unquantified Requirements' and hated it immediately. The ink blotted. I left the next line empty rather than pretend I could finish the category before supper. Years later, I found myself adding notes to the same page under different names. I have never enjoyed an inventory that records my ignorance so accurately.

## 1879

### E04-003 | The Letter in Three Hands | 1879-01

Voss returned my paper annotated in three hands, two inks and a pencil so sharp it had torn the margin. I objected that an argument ought at least to show its owner. Clara suggested that three naturalists might simply have worked at one table, a thought disappointingly plausible. The annotations challenged my interpretation of a creature moving toward a favorable condition as proof of preference. I wrote eleven pages in defense, discovered that the ninth contradicted the third, and sent two pages with additional observations. Voss wrote 'Useful' beneath the amended table. Heller had once bestowed the same parsimonious blessing. I had not expected a young botanist to revive the old irritation quite so efficiently. I asked her for the next objection before supper.

### E14-013 | The Experiment That Would Not Flatter Me | 1879-02

Voss arrived with botanical drawings which showed a relation between growth and enclosure less straightforward than I had predicted. Clara prepared three controlled vessels, each supplied with the same medium but with a different arrangement of light. I favored the narrowest one on aesthetic grounds: the subject stood at its center like a figure upon a stage. It also produced the poorest growth. Voss repeated the work, obtained the same disappointment, and wrote the measurements without comment. I claimed the arrangement had been damaged in transit. Clara pointed out that the alleged damage was present in all three vessels. After supper I revised the drawing, leaving a narrower stage only for observations that required isolation. Voss never asked me to confess error. She sent a cleaner drawing by post and marked the relevant dimensions in green ink. It was a superior form of victory. Had she demanded an apology, I should perhaps have defended the foolish apparatus another year.

### E26-004 | The Vinegar Cloth | 1879-02

Marta arrived with comparative observations and stopped in the doorway. She asked why the northern chamber smelled of vinegar. I had ceased to notice it. A cleaning cloth had been left behind a cabinet; its sourness occupied the narrow room though nothing had broken. I called it irrelevant. Marta opened a window and two small inhabitants shifted away from the draft. Clara closed the window and removed the cloth instead. Both women demanded another observation after the air had settled. The second movement was much less dramatic. I disliked their refusal to let me classify the first as a reaction to the odor. In my private account I conceded that becoming accustomed to a condition does not render it neutral. The cloth made an excellent household joke and an inconvenient methodological example.

### E23-001 | The Scent That Returned | 1879-03

Voss placed a sprig of thyme beside a cracked drain. A small inhabitant who had been motionless crossed the chamber in a sudden hurry. I called it an interest in botany. Voss reminded me that the same smell had been present when a vessel leaked the previous summer. I searched the old entries and found an account of frightened movement recorded as an effect of temperature. The room still smelled of wet plaster; verdigris showed beneath the copper basin. I had been so pleased with the neatness of my old explanation that I had failed to notice the neighboring detail. We restored the original arrangement without moving the cutting, then removed the cutting alone. The response changed. My notebook acquired the title 'Associations Not of My Choosing.' ### E04-004 | The Picnic No One Had Planned | 1879-07

Voss came through Ingolstadt on business, and Clara arranged for all four of us to eat outdoors as if such a thing required no justification. Lenz brought eggs wrapped in newspaper. Voss carried a specimen box and placed it beneath the shade, then proceeded to speak for an hour about village gardens rather than botany. I disliked how swiftly she and Clara agreed that my appetite could not be trusted. Lenz spent most of the afternoon watching his daughter run at the edge of the water. For a moment I envied him the ease with which he could be both craftsman and father. Then the child asked me how a clock knew it was morning, and I was permitted to answer a question more pleasant than anything in my correspondence. ### E17-004 | A Bed for a Visitor | 1879-09

Reiss came to the tower with a damaged valise and asked whether he might stay two nights while a difficult question was settled at his school. I gave him the west room, chiefly because it was the only chamber in which the roof was not leaking. He told me an acquaintance had threatened to expose the correspondence between himself and another man. I asked whether the letters were authentic. He said they were. I advised him not to purchase silence from someone who could demand further payment. He pointed out that I possessed a locked gate, independent income sufficient for my habits, and almost no institutional reputation left to lose. He did not. I sent Mathilde a note asking her to retain his papers in a sealed envelope, should he choose to deposit them. Reiss went away without using the offer. For weeks afterward I checked the west-room window latch before bed, although the room was empty. I did not regard myself as responsible for his security. The latch received considerable attention nevertheless.

### E23-002 | The Keeper of the False Relic | 1879-10

A gentleman offered me a splinter of bone in a silver mount, assuring me that it had moved beneath a medium's hand. I took it to Georg because he knew old cases and their ordinary dishonest repairs. He turned the mount over and recognized the velvet beneath it as a strip his father had sold three summers earlier. The bone may have been venerable; the stage upon which it performed was not. I purchased the affair for less than its owner expected and placed it in a cabinet beside a broken clock and a portrait whose eyes appeared to follow visitors. The gentlemen who inspected these objects invariably chose the relic as the most impressive. I allowed the deception to proceed for a minute before showing the stitching. This was not kindness. Their faces became much more interesting after the revelation.

## 1880

### E04-005 | The Mirror Kept Covered | 1880-02

The Devil had for years bowed to a looking glass with such magnificent affectation that I could not bear to deprive him of it. Voss uncovered the glass at irregular intervals and found that he sometimes approached, sometimes attended instead to the King's disturbances. She proposed that he liked movement rather than his own reflection. I objected that the two are scarcely enemies: vanity is often an appetite for a moving likeness. Our first arrangements could not separate the possibilities. We devised a less flattering test with changes of light and angle, and the creature continued to frustrate us. I retained his name. When he bowed again I bowed back, to Voss's evident despair. One need not resolve the mechanism of a performance to appreciate its style.

### E20-010 | The Uniform Face | 1880-02

Lenz brought a finely graduated gauge and asked why I had copied the same facial proportions into six designs. I said that an intelligent artist does not abandon a solution because it has proved successful. He spread the results on the table: six refined faces, none possessed of the ordinary signs of continued life. The proportions were not the only variable, as he knew, but they were the part I had refused to reconsider. We worked a week altering the drawings. The revised shape offended me and performed no better. I ought to have been pleased that the evidence spared my aesthetic principle. The patron selected the finest drawing, naturally, a specimen with a serene face and impossible hands. Mathilde noticed a smudge over one figure in an old table. I had read it as a three. The line beneath suggested an eight. ### E21-005 | Mesmerism without a Master | 1888-09

### E23-016 | The Instrument No One Could Inherit | 1888-10

### E18-004 | The Agreement We Did Not Sign | 1888-12

### E26-008 | The Handle after the Funeral | 1888-12

The repaired instrument made its altered click and I reached for the handle. It remained smooth where a dead colleague's hand had worn it. I had made her, or at least had given the conditions in which she came to be; I had not instructed her to form expectations about me. ### E24-008 | The Glass that Could Not Grow | 1892-11

I had enlarged the chamber with extraordinary attention to proportion. Its supports were stronger, its seals finer, and its occupant no nearer to remaining viable beyond the familiar interval. The fourth failure was accompanied by a shattered rim that cut Lenz's finger. He wrapped it in clean linen and went home without waiting for my apology. Voss said the next attempt would reproduce the same ignorance at a greater cost. I replied that the scale of an error is sometimes what reveals its nature. She asked what new variable I proposed to change. I could name none. We suspended the series. For weeks I passed the large empty vessel and felt the peculiar insult of an instrument that had performed all its duties while the hypothesis collapsed. I did not dismantle it. ### E21-010 | The Face in the Dirty Window | 1892-12

I was climbing the eastern stair after midnight when a pale face appeared against the window, seeming to look inward from a height no man could have reached. I stopped with one hand on the latch. When the rain shifted, so did the features. There had been no visitor. Adler, arriving behind me, asked why I had gone so white. I said the stair was cold. He stood with me while we watched the face dissolve into streams of water and the faint reflection of my own lamp. Later I drew the pattern in the same book that held my glass-door dream. I knew perfectly well what I had seen. What troubled me was the interval in which I had not known. I did not give that interval the dignity of a ghost story.

### E15-012 | The Sale I Was Prepared to Force | 1893-02

An estate catalogue offered a rare regulator clock with a mechanism I believed could improve our timed observations. ### E05-009 | The Letter I Did Send | 1893-03

I had drafted many letters to Henry in the years since his last reply, almost all unsent. This one was brief. ### E12-010 | The Envelope from Henry | 1893-04

I had sent Henry a letter in March after years of attempting to compose one that would be neither a plea nor an indictment. ### E26-011 | A Larger Room in the Chest | 1893-04

### E24-009 | The Printed Marriage Notice | 1893-05

I came to the shop for proofs and saw a marriage announcement in the newspaper stacked for wrapping. ### E08-001 | The Letter with Two Endings | 1893-06

The letter I had at last sent to Henry in March left behind two discarded endings. One asked him to write about the work. ### E21-011 | The Second Life of a Broken Jar | 1893-07

A repaired jar arrived with a scratch across one side. I was preparing a complaint when Anna observed that the mark fell exactly across the point we had difficulty locating on successive evenings. She used it to align the observations, and the measurements grew more consistent. There was nothing magical in this improvement. The scratch simply remained where the other marks had shifted. ### E08-002 | The Photograph of a Hand | 1893-09

Anna took a photograph of her father's injured hand resting beside a newly finished glass joint. She intended it as a study of texture and asked whether I wished to examine the plate. ### E05-010 | The Sound Behind the Wall | 1893-11

A rhythmic tapping began behind the west chamber wall, usually after midnight. I assumed some animal had made a nest. Anna proposed that the newly fitted drain was transmitting movement from the outer gutter. Lenz tapped the pipe and reproduced the sound almost exactly. We wrapped the pipe and shifted her vessel by a hand's breadth. The rhythm ceased and the dancing returned, though I recorded no claim of causation beyond the sequence. ### E24-010 | An Honest Catalogue of Imperfections | 1893-12

I wished to prepare a catalogue of the little court suitable for a visitor who had offered to purchase drawings. The Queen's profile deserved first place, followed by the Ballerina and the King. Voss sent a note about an English editor who wanted the Queen's history reduced to three columns. Mathilde set a sample page. Clara informed me that she intended to reduce her hours and transfer much of the business to an assistant. I immediately asked whether the tower could still obtain its customary supplies. ### E24-011 | The Dreams I Could Not Manufacture | 1894-07

The glass door had appeared in my sleep often enough that I began to resent its independence. ### E26-012 | A Whistle through the Dream | 1894-09

### E15-013 | The Name on the Seating Card | 1894-10

Voss persuaded me to attend a dinner at which an optical society intended to honor several instrument makers. Julian sat three places away. ### E08-004 | The Surgeon in a Yellow Book | 1894-12

A London volume containing Machen's *The Great God Pan* reached me through Mathilde's book trade. Clara had begun opening her shop late on Thursdays. I went at the former hour and found the front door locked, an event which my dignity ought to have endured without comment. Reuter asked whether the little people might become exhausted by repeated observation. I answered that I had already limited the trials. News of Oscar Wilde's conviction reached us through the foreign papers. Mathilde laid a report upon her table and said that a man's entire life could be reduced to the word assigned him by a court. ### E05-013 | A New Instrument, an Old Bias | 1895-10

Faber's new timing instrument could divide an interval with exquisite delicacy. I used it upon the King's movements and announced improved regularity. Julian had arranged an excursion in winter, a week away from instruments and the incessant improvisations by which I kept my household running. I had agreed months before. On the fourth attempt the Ballerina danced. I declared the experiment successful and sent Clara the news. She wrote that she was glad to be relieved of a duty I had apparently assigned to her for life. The old music pages remained beside the piano after Anna brought a clean copy. I could have put them away. For several winters I had known exactly when Clara would arrive by the sound of her boots below the stairs, and a new pianist could not reproduce that particular prelude.

## 1897

### E08-010 | A Young Woman's Signature | 1897-02

Anna asked that new shipping agreements carry her own signature rather than her father's. Jakob appeared entirely willing, which I found both generous and surprisingly careless. He said she had been managing the photographic records and a portion of the accounts for some time. I proposed adding my name as guarantor. Anna thanked me and declined. The first crate sent under her signature arrived with its contents arranged more sensibly than in any of Jakob's earlier packages, although I was careful not to say so within his hearing. A second came late because she had accepted another commission. I complained. She forwarded a copy of the terms showing that I had ordered no priority. I paid for expedited service next time. Her independence was excellent when directed against inefficiency in others and altogether less charming when it cost me money.

### E21-015 | The Account of a Stranger's Dream | 1897-04

Frieda wrote that one of her clients had dreamed of standing outside a transparent room, unable to reach a handle upon the other side. I went to Munich to hear the description. The client did not appear; Frieda gave the account from her notebook, with names concealed. It resembled my dream enough to make me cold, but differed in the position of the door, the company within and even the feeling the dreamer described. I had not told Frieda my earlier dream, so far as I could remember. Yet I had spoken often enough of glass to make accidental resemblance a less heroic explanation than she preferred. I copied the account exactly, paid the fare home, and dreamed of a locked wooden cupboard that night. I did not tell her that last detail.

### E24-015 | The Lesson Received by the Devil | 1897-04

The Devil looked splendid in his little black cape, a figure made for defying expectations, which ought to have warned me against requiring submission. I had arranged a simple demonstration for two visitors. He was to cross the platform when I tapped a chipped saucer. He remained still. I tapped again, rather harder. Anna moved to the opposite side of the chamber and he approached her at once. The guests laughed. I assured them that the arrangement had tested attraction to familiar caretakers, though such had not been my purpose. After their departure I repeated the situation without an audience and obtained results sufficiently variable to forbid easy interpretation. I stopped demonstrating obedience. It was an ugly decision, since the little subject's refusal had become the most popular feature of my collection. Perhaps his judgment of the performance was better than mine.

### E05-016 | The London Address | 1897-08

Agnes Bell, a London translator acquainted with Voss, asked for an account that could be separated from its author's reputation. An admirably rude request. I sent her the Queen's avoidance of certain instruments, including observations which weakened my favored interpretation. She translated the report without strengthening its conclusions, a restraint rare enough to merit praise. At a meeting, someone inquired whether my professorial title remained valid. Bell replied that dismissal did not render a man's measurements false. Later, over a supper notable chiefly for its bad fish, she said she had defended the evidence rather than its author. I answered that this was precisely why I had trusted her with it. She seemed surprised. I was not. I had learned, after some expense, to prefer an ally who could distinguish me from my legend.

### E08-011 | The Count in the New Novel | 1897-09

Bell dispatched Stoker's newly published *Dracula* with a note praising its assemblage of diaries, letters, and mechanical recordings. I expected a melodrama of blood and superstition. I found instead that the most persuasive passages were those in which imperfect witnesses compared their accounts, corrected their errors, and discovered the limits of any solitary observer. A hateful lesson to learn from a book with a vampire in it. Mathilde preferred the passages about the typewriter and the speed with which Mina organized a confused record. I objected that filing papers was a lesser art than generating knowledge. She asked where my knowledge would be after the next fire. I handed her the oldest box of loose correspondence and permitted another index. I refused to call it a tribute to Mr. Stoker, who had enough admirers already.

### E08-012 | The Portrait Not for Sale | 1897-10

A dealer offered a handsome price for Anna's photograph of me beside the tower doorway. He described the ears as a marvel of physiognomy and proposed selling the print in a cabinet of monsters. I said his cabinet was surely already occupied by critics. Mathilde advised that refusing publication would merely increase the rumor. Anna observed that the negative belonged to her, and that she did not wish to supply a curiosity stall. The decision was therefore taken by a young woman whose professional property I had almost forgotten to recognize. We asked the dealer to purchase an ordinary photograph of the tower, without me in it. He declined. Anna produced another print for our records, framed the original view according to her own taste, and kept the negative. I called this a tolerable resolution. She called it hers.

### E26-014 | The Wick and the Queen | 1897-10

The old lamp smoked below the western curtain. The smell of lamp oil reached me at once; I saw again the patch beneath the iron stand, Clara's hands and the vessel I had been more anxious to save than the room. The present flame was steady. Anna observed that the Queen had moved behind her paper shelter before the wick smoked. I wanted the odor to explain the retreat. Anna reminded me that the western draft had changed. We moved the lamp, observed again and found no consistent response. I kept my thumb away from the hot collar, a small triumph after twenty-one years. In the margin I wrote that the scent had summoned fear and the Queen had sought cover; neither established a causal relation to the other. At supper the smoke seemed lodged in my coat. I opened the window.

### E15-015 | An Answer to the Count | 1897-11

Agnes Bell wrote after reading the English vampire novel whose diaries and telegrams Mathilde had praised. She asked whether the monster in that book frightened me. I answered that the villain's appetite was ordinary enough; what interested me was the carefully organized coalition assembled to oppose him. Each member possessed incomplete knowledge, and the case advanced only when they allowed their fragments to form an account none could produce alone. Agnes replied that my admiration for the hunters was a surprising development in a man celebrated for preferring his own company. I objected that collaboration was not democracy. Her next letter contained a single line asking how I distinguished the two when a collaborator disagreed. I spent a week preparing a rigorous answer and eventually sent two pages on governance of scientific work. She returned them with three precise questions and a note that she would be away for Christmas. I kept those questions open beside the blueprint of the larger laboratory. Even a badly written English adventure, it seemed, could furnish a troublesome model for the arrangement of minds.

### E18-009 | Science and the Petition | 1897-11

Mathilde showed me a notice of the campaign associated with Magnus Hirschfeld and the new Scientific-Humanitarian Committee. Reiss, writing from abroad, had enclosed a few observations about the circulation of Ulrichs's work. I recognized the ambition to place scientific evidence against a penal statute, although I disliked the confidence with which some writers believed a sufficiently neat explanation of desire would compel humane conduct. Scientists could supply facts; lawmakers might still choose to be cruel. Mathilde asked whether I would sign a petition. I said that I would first examine its wording, which was true. I then neglected the matter for several weeks, which was another truth and less dignified. When I finally sent my name, I did so through her, with a note correcting two technical statements in the accompanying appeal. She told me that signatures had been requested rather than a journal referee's report. I replied that both deserved scrutiny. I kept Reiss's letter with the copy of the statute from 1871, two papers sharing a drawer despite an interval of twenty-six years.

### E12-014 | The Voice in the Other Letter | 1897-12

Bell wrote from London after our exchange about printed accounts of the extraordinary. She said my objections to the new novel's elaborate collection of diaries were persuasive, but that no single narrator could know everything his companions had concealed. Mathilde, sorting proofs beside the fire, asked whether Bell knew I had written a private record of my own work. I said my record was sufficient for its purpose. She said it contained very little of what the others thought while I was speaking. I suggested that their thoughts belonged to their own notebooks. That evening I attempted to reconstruct an argument with Voss from memory and discovered that I could reproduce my position in exquisite detail while failing to recall her first sentence. I wrote to ask her for her version. The reply corrected a minor fact and an entire motive. I kept both accounts. Neither was particularly flattering.

### E21-016 | The Diagram without a Person | 1897-12

I spread a large drawing beneath the lamp. Every line was elegant, every joint connected, and every proportion chosen with uncommon care. Anna placed beside it the diary of a living miniature whose odd little habits had survived three alterations of its chamber. The drawing answered what I had intended the being to be. The diary answered what it was doing now. It struck me that an anatomist might admire the first while remaining ignorant of the second. I did not conclude that design was unimportant. Far from it. The design had made the life possible. But the subject had occupied the intervening years with activities I had not specified. I set the diary beside the diagram rather than beneath it and called the arrangement an improvement in filing.

## 1898

### E08-013 | The Page Kappel Never Numbered | 1898-01

While Clara and Mathilde sorted the inherited papers, we found an unnumbered sheet in Kappel's hand. It contained a list of things borrowed from me and never returned: a lens, a set of maps, one blue ribbon, and an umbrella. The last item made me think of the scholar whose obituary I had kept years before. Clara laughed at the ribbon. I claimed no recollection of it, although I distinctly remembered binding Henry's first drawings with something blue. The list offered no proof. I copied the items into the secondary archive and resisted turning the sheet into a sentimental relic. Nevertheless I kept the original on the table all afternoon, beside Kappel's old magnifying glass. That evening I used his numbering convention for the first time without altering it to suit my preferences. It was a sufficient memorial, more useful than flowers.

### E05-017 | What the Queen Had Not Forgotten | 1898-02

Anna set a new wooden stand beside the Queen's glass. The little sovereign withdrew at once, much as she had years earlier when a certain clamp approached. Anna noticed the resemblance of outline. We tried an innocuous duplicate and observed another retreat. Voss rightly objected that shape alone did not establish remembered experience. I made the objection sharper: without a known account of the intervening years, we could not say what had been preserved or how. But the possibility was intoxicating. I searched the blue ledger and found my old description of the first disturbance: 'trivial.' I had dismissed an incident which might have endured within the creature long after it had ceased to occupy my mind. I underlined the word, resisting the temptation to improve the record. It was not my finest sentence. It might yet prove important.

### E21-017 | A Door Left Unopened | 1898-03

Adler asked whether I wanted him to remain for the coming season or whether I would continue scheduling companionship between experiments. I replied with a joke about the reliability of instruments compared with the rest of creation. He did not laugh. We stood at the landing where a brass handle caught the afternoon light, and I recalled, most unhelpfully, the door from my recurring dream. I could have asked him to stay. I could have said that I did not know how much of my work I was prepared to rearrange. Instead I discussed the weather. He went down the stairs and left his gloves on the table, then returned for them without a word. For weeks afterward I worked later into the night than the experiments required. One can call that industry if one does not care to be exact.

### E08-014 | The Cup with the Chipped Rim | 1898-04

Clara poured tea into the cup with the chipped rim because it was, she said, the one I always took. I had no memory of choosing it. Voss arrived later with seedlings in a paper wrap and remarked that I placed the cup at the same angle whenever I read. We tested the assertion twice, which was absurd, and discovered that I did so even when the handle was on the wrong side. Clara laughed until she coughed. I objected that an habitual motion was not a theory of character. Neither woman suggested otherwise. By evening the seedlings stood beneath the garden wall and my cup was in the washing bowl. I asked Clara whether she intended to replace it. She said not while it remained useful. On my next visit it was waiting in its familiar place. I regarded the matter as settled and was privately relieved.

### E18-010 | The Terms of an Open Door | 1898-05

Julian visited on his way through Bavaria, carrying no instrument at all. I regarded this as a disarming omission. We walked in the garden, where the nettles had appropriated a path I had intended to repair for years. He spoke of the life he had made in Vienna, of friends whose names were not familiar to me, and of a younger restorer to whom he had entrusted part of his business. I discovered that I had been treating his absence as a temporary condition which my next letter might end. He said he cared for me, but would not arrange his remaining years around a door which opened only when my experiments permitted. I objected to the theatricality of his metaphor and knew immediately that it was mine he had learned. He did not threaten to sever the connection. He asked only that I cease describing another person's independence as a betrayal. After his departure I had the neglected path cut back. I wrote to him describing the work, not pretending it had been done for an optical demonstration. He sent a short note asking whether the nettles would return. They have, repeatedly.

### E05-018 | The Visit to Clara's Garden | 1898-07

Clara had taken to keeping herbs and bees behind her house. I visited one July afternoon without needing a bottle, an instrument, or a favor. She asked my opinion of a sickly plant. I pronounced on its leaves, twice and incorrectly, until she lifted a paving stone and showed me how the roots had been crowded. We moved the stone, drank warm tea, and watched bees make an industry of the thyme. I spoke of the lives I had brought forth. Clara replied that her garden had never once obeyed her merely because she planted it. I said my case was rather different. She handed me the watering can without further dispute. The plant recovered. On my next visit I brought a drawing of its roots, and she used the reverse of the paper to write a shopping list.

### E08-015 | The Man Who Wanted a Cure | 1898-08

A merchant whose son had lost the use of an arm sought me out through Reuter. He had heard that I made living bodies where ordinary doctors found only damage. I was tempted to offer a grand account of my accomplishments. Reuter asked me to explain what I could actually do for the boy. I had no treatment to offer, and the father was not interested in a lecture on the future of science. We discussed a brace being prepared by a local craftsman, then let him go. Later, Voss said she had never heard me speak so little to a paying visitor. I replied that he had come seeking medicine, not philosophy, and I did not possess the commodity he required. His disappointment was sharper than most committee judgments. I do not claim it improved me. I remember that he thanked Reuter and did not thank me.

### E15-016 | The Shape of an Empty Place | 1898-09

Anna measured the west chamber for a vessel large enough to permit a creature to move without continually confronting the glass. Voss argued that the design must distinguish free movement from mere absence of obstacles. I proposed several mechanisms and then rejected most as impractical. Leonie came in the afternoon, slower upon the stairs than formerly, carrying a square of black fabric intended to suppress reflections during photography. She spread it across the floor to show how the chamber would appear when its center was empty. The room looked unexpectedly severe. I remarked that an empty stage was still a stage, and that one could not call an experiment complete merely because the apparatus waited beautifully for its subject. Voss asked who would be allowed to enter such a chamber and under what conditions. I began to answer for the hypothetical creature, then stopped and drew two doors instead of one. Anna corrected their measurements. Leonie folded the cloth and went downstairs before the work was finished. I heard her pause upon the landing, and I listened until the steps resumed. There would be time, I told myself, to ask her to visit again.

### E21-018 | The Chosen Scar | 1898-09

Anna brought the newest chamber and asked whether I wished to hide the repair seam beneath a narrow gilt band. It would have been easy. The chamber was otherwise handsome and its little occupant remained in good health. I held the gold pin against the joint, imagining how the band would complete the line. Voss observed that the seam gave a later observer a reason to ask what had happened. I had not invited her to supply symbolism to an engineering decision. Nevertheless I refused the gilt band. The repair remained exposed, a rough crescent interrupting the polished glass. I could still have made it prettier. I cannot pretend I ceased to want that. I was simply no longer certain that concealment would count as perfection.

### E26-015 | Three Odors in an Unfinished Room | 1898-09

I laid out a coal receipt, the account of the smoking lamp and the record of the second sitting. The papers smelled chiefly of ink and dust. Yet while reading I recovered wet coal beneath Rosa's boots, greasy lamp oil in the lower laboratory and the excessive rosewater of the Munich lodging. They arrived out of order. The lamp brought Clara and embarrassment; the coal brought cold and appetite; the rosewater brought uncertainty over a misspelled name. Marta asked whether I meant to classify these as evidence about the original events or about my present remembering. I declared the distinction intolerably modern for a tower without modern conveniences. She drew two columns anyway. I wrote verifiable records in the first and present recollections in the second. By evening the second column was longer. I did not burn it.

### E12-015 | The Mirror Turned to the Wall | 1898-10

Preparations for the larger laboratory had revived my habit of performing grand conclusions before the figures could sustain them. Voss brought a design objection; Anna brought an estimate; Clara brought the Queen's recent record of avoiding particular instruments. I had intended to present a comprehensive account of my progress. Instead we spent the afternoon moving a mirror away from the vessel and observing whether the Queen returned to her usual ledge. She did, though not at once. Anna made a note of the interval. Voss said I was capable of treating an uncertain result with patience when it happened to occupy a glass jar. I objected that the jar was a controlled world and the larger apparatus an unfinished one. After they left, I began the address again before the empty workbench. It sounded magnificent. I folded it into a drawer and kept the Queen's observation sheet upon the desk. Before bed I took the folded speech out again, read its opening two lines, and returned it to the drawer without correcting them.

### E25-030 | The Page I No Longer Folded Away | 1898-10

The newspaper had grown brittle where it had been folded in Paris. It no longer opened without a warning at the crease. I spread it on the desk and weighted the corners with two glass stops, as I once weighted the pages of a laboratory record. There was no need to read it. I knew the questions, my evasions, the gracious opening, and the sentence about a will that could not govern itself. For many years I had blamed the article for showing the university the sort of man it ought to fear. Then I blamed myself for giving an intelligent reporter the very answers he was bound to print. Both accounts were too simple. The faculty had its reasons, some petty, some legitimate; I had made my own injuries, and others had been inflicted upon me. Jolivet had listened carefully. I still remembered his hand beside the diagram and the pleasure of Paris before any criticism. I replaced the folded sheet in the blue ledger. I did not forgive him, for I no longer knew which of his actions required forgiveness. I did not discard him either.

### E05-019 | The Scale Model of an Impossible Room | 1898-11

I laid out plans for a laboratory large enough to support a form of life the public could no longer dismiss as a charming smallness. Lenz measured the stairwell and laughed in a manner which I have never encouraged. Anna constructed a paper model and demonstrated that the central apparatus could not round the entrance passage. Voss asked what observations justified its dimensions. I had estimates, hopes, and a theory still too young to be publicly insulted. I did not order the construction. The model went beside the catalogue of failures, its paper walls carefully repaired where Anna had cut them to demonstrate the problem. It was not a surrender. I continued drawing alternative entrances long after the others had gone, one candle burning down into the saucer. The larger creature existed already as a demand upon my imagination, if nowhere else.

### E24-016 | The Room That Needed No Curtain | 1898-11

By the last autumn before the news from Henry, the western curtain had acquired a seam where I had repaired it years earlier. I stood beside Clara while Anna cleaned the window, and the Queen moved behind a little paper shelter I had never successfully improved. The chamber required new glass, new seals, and an accountant willing to forgive our figures. What it did not require, at least that evening, was another audience. I left the curtain down until the lamps were lit and forbade visitors during the following hour. Clara asked whether I had become tender. I informed her that interruption affected repeatability. She accepted the explanation with a smile so small it was barely an observable event. I can still summon the sound of the glass being cleaned. I cannot say why that ordinary noise has survived in memory when so many grander apparatuses have vanished.

### E08-016 | A Place for the Unwritten | 1898-12

Anna's paper model of the impossible larger laboratory occupied half my desk. Beside it stood the little witness book, the false engraving with its invented crown, Kappel's unnumbered sheet, and a square of brown paper that Mathilde had labelled 'unverified.' Voss objected to using that label for a drawer full of ideas rather than observations. We agreed upon 'uncompleted,' a word with the merit of recording neither failure nor success. I put Henry's oldest drawing into the drawer and then took it out again. It belonged with the letters, where its author remained unmistakable. Anna measured the window before she left and said a larger table could be carried up the stairs if we removed its legs. I asked her to prepare a sketch, not a purchase order. The Queen remained upon her favored ledge while we extinguished the lamps. I had no resolution to announce. There was work enough for the following year, and no one present mistook that for a promise of immortality.

## 1899

### E05-020 | A Rumor with a Familiar Name | 1899-03

Bell enclosed a newspaper clipping concerning a Frankenstein said to have restored motion to dead flesh and brought catastrophe upon himself. A surname in print is a poor identification, especially when journalists are permitted to decorate it. I read the report twice for names, witnesses, and inconsistencies, then searched for Henry's old unsigned drawings. His scheme of assembly lay beside my notes on growth, as it had thirty-four years before beneath a smoking lamp. Voss asked if I intended to write him. I answered that I first intended to discover whether the man in the report was alive. She watched me longer than the answer deserved. I was not certain whether I wished to see my former pupil vindicated, or to discover the one thing he might have done without me. I did know that I disliked learning of it through a newspaper.

### E05-021 | The News from the Windmill | 1899-06

The second account came in June, and with it a confirmation from a correspondent not given to the more picturesque forms of falsehood. Henry Frankenstein had brought a being to life from dead flesh. The creature had survived the fire at a windmill. Henry was alive. I entered the claims, their sources, and the matters still concealed from me in the blue ledger. The diagram of separate organs lay open on the table, its corners curled with age. Henry had pursued assembly while I pursued growth, and there, at last, was a result no committee could dismiss by refusing to inspect it. Anna asked whether I would seek him. Voss asked what I would want when I found him. I closed the travelling case and looked through the east window toward the road. I offered neither woman an answer. The Queen moved upon her ledge behind me. I remained in the tower that night, with the old drawings spread beneath my hand.
